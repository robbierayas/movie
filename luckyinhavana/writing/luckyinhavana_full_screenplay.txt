# ACT 1 - THE EXILE KING

----------------------------------------
# Scene 1 - The Deportation
----------------------------------------

FADE IN:

EXT. NEW YORK CITY - DAY (FEBRUARY 1946)

Aerial view of Manhattan. Gray winter light. The city sprawls beneath low clouds.

MONTAGE begins. Archival feel -- grainy footage, newspaper clippings, courtroom sketches, mug shots. Over it all, a voice reads with the flat authority of a federal official entering charges into record.

                    OFFICIAL (V.O.)
          Salvatore Lucania. Also known as Charles
          Luciano. Also known as Lucky Luciano. Born
          November twenty-fourth, eighteen ninety-seven,
          Lercara Friddi, Sicily.

Close on a mug shot. Young Lucky, defiant.

                    OFFICIAL (V.O.)
          Nineteen sixteen: arrested, narcotics
          possession. Nineteen nineteen: arrested,
          illegal sale of narcotics. Nineteen twenty-six:
          arrested, robbery.

Quick cuts: newspaper headlines, police booking photos, handcuffs.

                    OFFICIAL (V.O.)
          Nineteen twenty-nine: kidnapped, beaten,
          left for dead on Staten Island. Survived.
          Hence the name.

A scar. A headline: "GANGSTER CHEATS DEATH."

                    OFFICIAL (V.O.)
          Nineteen thirty-six: convicted on sixty-two
          counts of compulsory prostitution. Sentenced
          to thirty to fifty years, Clinton State Prison.

Courtroom sketch. Prison gates closing. A man in grays walking a corridor.

                    OFFICIAL (V.O.)
          February ninth, nineteen forty-six: sentence
          commuted. Condition of release: immediate
          deportation to Italy. Permanent. Salvatore
          Lucania is hereby prohibited from entering
          the United States of America for the
          remainder of his natural life.

EXT. FEDERAL COURTHOUSE - NEW YORK - DAY

February cold. The courthouse steps are packed with PRESS PHOTOGRAPHERS jostling for position. Flashbulbs pop in staccato
bursts.

The doors open.

LUCKY LUCIANO, 49, emerges flanked by two FEDERAL ESCORTS. He wears a dark overcoat, hat tilted at a precise angle, and
the expression of a man who has won something even in losing. No handcuffs. No chains. He walks like he's leaving a
business meeting.

The press surges. Flashbulbs explode. Lucky doesn't flinch. He descends the steps with measured dignity, eyes forward.

Across the steps, partially obscured by the crowd --

JACK BRENNAN, late 30s, stands with his hands in his coat pockets. Hard jaw, harder eyes. He watches Lucky descend with
an intensity that goes beyond professional interest. This is personal.

Beside him, DIRECTOR WILLIAM HARTLEY, late 50s, watches the same scene with the detached satisfaction of a man checking
an item off a list. Silver-haired, impeccable posture, government-issue confidence.

                    HARTLEY
          There he goes. Shipped back where he came
          from. Case closed, Jack.

Brennan doesn't respond immediately. His eyes track Lucky through the crowd.

                    BRENNAN
          He doesn't look like a man who lost.

                    HARTLEY
          He's being deported. What would you call it?

                    BRENNAN
          A technicality. He did ten years of a fifty-year
          sentence. Ten years.

Lucky reaches the bottom of the steps. A black car waits. He pauses, adjusts his hat, and glances back at the courthouse
-- just for a moment. Then he's in the car, and the door closes.

                    HARTLEY
          The man is going to Italy, Brennan.
          Across the Atlantic Ocean. What's he going
          to do from there?

                    BRENNAN
          You ever know a man like Luciano to stop?

Hartley gives him a look -- not unkind, but dismissive.

                    HARTLEY
          Go home. Get some rest. It's over.

Brennan watches the car pull away from the curb and merge into traffic.

Brennan, quiet, to himself.

                    BRENNAN
          My brother thought heroin would stop too.

Hartley is already walking toward his own car. He doesn't hear it. Or doesn't want to.

EXT. NEW YORK HARBOR - DAY

The S.S. LAURA KEENE at dock. A passenger vessel, modest by ocean liner standards. Porters load luggage. Officials check
paperwork.

Lucky walks up the gangway with IGEA LISSONI, young, beautiful, wrapped in a fur coat against the February wind. She
holds his arm. He walks with purpose, not looking back.

At the top of the gangway, Lucky pauses. He turns.

The New York skyline stretches behind the harbor -- the city he controlled for twenty years. Steel and stone and smoke
against a winter sky.

Lucky takes it in. His expression betrays nothing -- but he stands there longer than a man who doesn't care would stand.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          On February ninth, nineteen forty-six,
          Salvatore Lucania boarded the S.S. Laura
          Keene bound for Italy. The United States
          government considered the matter settled.

The ship's horn sounds. Dock lines are cast off. The Laura Keene pulls away from the pier, widening the gap between the
ship and the city.

EXT. NEW YORK HARBOR - CONTINUOUS

Brennan stands at the edge of the dock, separate from the small crowd of onlookers. He watches the ship recede. The wind
whips his coat.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          They were wrong.

The ship grows smaller against the harbor. The skyline looms. Brennan stands motionless, a man watching a mistake unfold
in real time.

SMASH CUT TO:

TITLE CARD: LUCKY IN HAVANA

FADE TO BLACK.

# Scene 2 - Italy
----------------------------------------

FADE IN:

EXT. LERCARA FRIDDI, SICILY - DAY (JUNE 1946)

A hill town baking under Italian sun. Stone houses, narrow streets, olive trees. A world that hasn't changed in
centuries.

SUPER: LERCARA FRIDDI, SICILY -- JUNE 1946

INT. LUCANIA FAMILY HOME - DAY

A modest kitchen. Whitewashed walls, a wooden table too large for the room, plates of food that could feed an army.
LUCKY'S MOTHER, 70s, small and weathered, sets down another plate. LUCKY'S SISTER fusses over her children in the corner.

Lucky sits at the head of the table in a suit that costs more than the house. He looks profoundly out of place -- a man
from another century visiting this one.

His UNCLE, 60s, pours wine from a clay jug and says something in Sicilian dialect. Lucky nods, smiles. His Italian is
rusty, accented with thirty years of New York.

                    LUCKY
          Si, si. E molto buono, Zio.

His mother reaches across and squeezes his hand, her eyes glistening. She speaks rapidly in Sicilian.

Lucky pats her hand. The smile on his face is real, but it doesn't quite reach his eyes.

                         LUCKY'S SISTER
                    (in accented English)
              She says you are too thin. America
              made you too thin.

                    LUCKY
          Tell her I'll eat everything she puts
          in front of me.

Laughter around the table. But it's the laughter of people who don't quite know each other. Lucky looks around at these
faces -- his blood, his family -- and they might as well be strangers.

EXT. LUCANIA FAMILY HOME - TERRACE - LATER

Lucky stands outside, smoking, looking out over the valley. Dusty hills, a church bell tower, donkeys on a path. Silence
so thick you can hear it.

His sister comes out, drying her hands on an apron.

                         LUCKY'S SISTER
              Mama wants to know how long you stay.

                    LUCKY
          I don't know.

                         LUCKY'S SISTER
              She has your old room ready. She's been
              getting it ready since they told us you
              were coming home.

Lucky takes a long drag. Looks at the view.

                    LUCKY
          This was never home, Rosa. I left when
          I was nine.

A beat. She nods, understanding more than she lets on.

                         LUCKY'S SISTER
              Then where is home?

He doesn't answer. She goes back inside. Lucky stands alone with the Sicilian hills and nothing else.

EXT. NAPLES STREET - DAY (WEEKS LATER)

SUPER: NAPLES -- AUGUST 1946

Lucky walks through the streets of Naples. His hair is different -- parted loosely, combed in the Italian style, softer
than the slicked-back look he wore in New York. He looks like a local. He moves through the crowds like a man pacing a
cell.

He passes a cafe where men play cards. A newsstand with Italian papers. A beggar on a corner. None of it matters to him.

INT. NAPLES APARTMENT - NIGHT

A rented apartment. Better than the family home but impersonal. Lucky sits at a small desk, an ashtray overflowing with
cigarette butts. Newspapers in English and Italian spread across the table. A comb sits beside the ashtray.

A PHONE RINGS. Lucky picks up.

                    LUCKY
          Yeah.

INTERCUT WITH:

INT. HOTEL ROOM - SOMEWHERE IN THE U.S. - NIGHT

MEYER LANSKY, 44, sharp-eyed and compact, sits on the edge of a hotel bed. A notebook open beside him, filled with
figures.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie. It's Meyer.

Lucky leans back. The sound of Lansky's voice changes something in his face -- the first real engagement we've seen since
he arrived in Italy. He picks up the comb idly, runs it through his hair as he listens.

                    LUCKY
          Meyer. How's the weather back home?

                    LANSKY
          I'm not back home. I'm calling from
          Havana.

                    LUCKY
          Cuba?

                    LANSKY
          Cuba. Listen to me. I've been down
          here three months. This place, Charlie --
          this place is wide open.

Lucky sits forward. His eyes sharpen. The comb keeps working through his hair -- absent, automatic.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          Batista's got the whole island running
          like a slot machine. You put money in,
          more money comes out. I've got thirty
          percent of the Hotel Nacional. The
          racetracks are gold. And there's nobody
          here to tell you no.

                    LUCKY
          What about the Americans? The Bureau?

                    LANSKY
          Ninety miles from Florida and they can't
          touch you. Different country, different
          laws, different everything. You're a
          free man in Cuba, Charlie. No parole
          officer, no surveillance, no conditions.

Lucky stands. He walks to the window, still combing. Naples spreads below -- dark, chaotic, irrelevant. His hair is
slicking back now, stroke by stroke, the Italian style disappearing.

                    LUCKY
          And Batista? He knows about me?

                    LANSKY
          Batista doesn't care who you are.
          Batista cares what you bring. And what
          you bring is money. American money,
          flowing through his island, keeping his
          government fat and happy.

                    LUCKY
          The old routes are drying up. China's
          finished.

                    LANSKY
          I know. That's the other thing. Cuba
          sits between Europe and every port on
          the Eastern Seaboard. You want to move
          product from the Mediterranean to New
          York? Tampa? New Orleans? Everything
          goes through Havana. It's the missing
          piece, Charlie.

A long beat. Lucky stares out the window, but he's not seeing Naples anymore. He's seeing something else entirely. His
hair is fully slicked back now -- the American style, the Lucky Luciano style. He didn't even notice himself doing it.

                    LUCKY
          How fast can you set it up?

                    LANSKY
          It's already set up. I've been waiting
          for you to make the call.

                    LUCKY
          Then stop waiting.
          (beat)
          I'm coming to Havana.

                    LANSKY
          I'll have everything ready. When?

                    LUCKY
          October. Give me a month to arrange
          the travel. Papers, passage, all of it
          quiet. Nobody knows until I'm there.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie -- it's going to be like the old
          days. Better than the old days.

                    LUCKY
          Nothing's like the old days, Meyer. But
          it doesn't have to be.

Lucky hangs up. He sets the comb down. Crushes out his cigarette.

He opens the wardrobe. Pulls out a dark suit -- the good one, the American one. White shirt. Silk tie. He dresses with
purpose, every movement deliberate.

Lucky steps in front of the mirror.

The man looking back is not the man who arrived in Italy. Hair slicked back sharp, dark suit cut clean, tie knotted tight.
The jawline, the eyes, the posture -- it's the Lucky Luciano from the photographs, from the headlines, from the wanted
posters. The king.

He adjusts his cuffs. Straightens the tie. Holds his own gaze for a long moment.

For the first time since arriving in Italy, Lucky Luciano looks like himself.

# Scene 3 - The Arrival
----------------------------------------

EXT. HAVANA AIRPORT - TARMAC - DAY (OCTOBER 29, 1946)

SUPER: HAVANA, CUBA -- OCTOBER 29, 1946

Caribbean light. Blinding white, nothing like Italy or New York. The heat shimmers off the tarmac. Palm trees line the
runway. A propeller plane taxis to a stop.

The cabin door opens.

LUCKY LUCIANO steps out into the Cuban sun. He pauses at the top of the stairs, removes his sunglasses, and takes in the
airport. He squints against the light, then smiles -- a small, private smile. A man arriving at a place he's already
decided to own.

He descends the stairs.

MEYER LANSKY waits at the bottom of the gangway, dressed in a light linen suit. He's been waiting in the heat but shows
no discomfort. His eyes are bright. Behind him, a black sedan idles.

Lucky reaches the tarmac. The two men look at each other.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie.

                    LUCKY
          Meyer.

They embrace -- brief, genuine, the handshake-to-hug of men who've known each other since childhood.

                    LANSKY
          How was the flight?

                    LUCKY
          Long. Bumpy. Beautiful at the end.
          That water coming in -- I never seen
          blue like that.

                    LANSKY
          Wait till you see the rest.

Lansky guides Lucky toward the terminal building, one hand on his shoulder.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          There's someone I want you to meet first.
          Government man. He's the one who made all
          of this happen on the Cuban side.

                    LUCKY
          He's clean?

                    LANSKY
          He's Cuban. Same thing.

INT. HAVANA AIRPORT - PRIVATE LOUNGE - CONTINUOUS

A small lounge off the main terminal. Ceiling fans turning slow. Two armchairs, a table with rum and cigars already laid
out.

MINISTER ALFREDO VALDES, early 50s, rises from his chair. He is impeccable -- white guayabera pressed to razor creases,
gold watch catching the light, the smile of a man who has been looking forward to this meeting. He crosses the room with
his hand extended.

                    VALDES
          Senor Luciano. Welcome to Cuba.

Lucky shakes his hand. He reads Valdes instantly -- a fixer, a man who speaks the language of accommodation.

                    LUCKY
          Minister Valdes. Meyer tells me you're
          the man who makes things work around here.

                    VALDES
          Cuba is a country that works very well
          for its friends. And you, senor, are a
          friend of Cuba.

Valdes gestures to the chairs. They sit. Lansky takes a seat slightly apart -- this is Valdes's show, and he's content to
watch.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          Your residency papers are complete. Signed
          and filed this morning. You are, as of today,
          a legal resident of the Republic of Cuba. No
          restrictions, no conditions, no reporting
          requirements.

He slides a leather folder across the table. Lucky opens it, glances at the documents.

                    LUCKY
          That's efficient.

                    VALDES
          We believe in hospitality.
          (beat)
          There is also the matter of your residence.
          General Perez Damera -- the chief of our
          armed forces -- has made his Miramar property
          available. It is a beautiful home. Ocean view,
          six bedrooms, full staff. A car will be at
          your disposal.

                    LUCKY
          The chief of the army is lending me his
          house?

                    VALDES
          He considers it an honor.

A beat. Lucky and Valdes look at each other. Two men who understand exactly how the world works.

                    LUCKY
          What do you need from me, Minister?

Valdes smiles. Warm. Completely honest in his dishonesty.

                    VALDES
          Only your friendship, senor. And the
          understanding that Cuba's hospitality is...
          reciprocal.

Lucky nods. He reaches inside his jacket and produces a thick envelope. He sets it on the table between them, casual as a
dinner check.

                    LUCKY
          Consider this a thank-you. For the welcome.

Valdes takes the envelope without looking inside it. It disappears into his jacket with practiced ease.

                    VALDES
          I have also arranged personal security
          for you. One of our best men.

Valdes turns toward the door and nods. A figure enters.

CARLOS DELGADO, early 40s, steps into the lounge. He wears a dark suit, clean-shaven, military bearing. He stands
straight, hands at his sides, waiting to be addressed. The face of a man who follows orders.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          Agent Carlos Delgado. Secret Police. He
          has been assigned to your personal detail.
          He will drive you, accompany you, ensure
          your safety and comfort. He is discreet
          and he is capable. You are in excellent
          hands.

Delgado steps forward.

                    DELGADO
          Senor Luciano. It is my assignment to
          ensure your security while you are in Cuba.

Lucky studies him. Reads the military posture, the careful formality, the eyes that are taking everything in.

                    LUCKY
          Carlos, is it?

                    DELGADO
          Yes, senor.

                    LUCKY
          You got a family, Carlos?

The question catches Delgado off guard. He hesitates.

                    DELGADO
          A wife and two daughters, senor.

                    LUCKY
          Good. A man with a family, I can trust.
          He's got something to go home to.

Lucky extends his hand. Delgado shakes it -- a firm, professional grip.

Lucky (cont'd) turns to Lansky.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          All right, Meyer. Show me my island.

EXT. HAVANA STREETS - DAY

The black sedan moves through Havana. Lucky sits in the back with Lansky. Delgado drives.

Through the windows: the city unfolds. Pastel-colored buildings, wrought-iron balconies, vintage cars, women in bright
dresses, a street band playing on a corner. Trees heavy with blossoms. Life everywhere -- loud, colorful, unapologetic.

                    LANSKY
          Miramar's the best neighborhood in
          Havana. Diplomats, generals, old money.
          The mansion's ten minutes from the
          Nacional, fifteen from the racetrack.

Lucky watches the city pass. Taking inventory. Every block, every face, every opportunity.

                    LUCKY
          It's something, Meyer.

                    LANSKY
          It's everything.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - CONTINUOUS

The sedan pulls through iron gates onto a tree-lined drive. The mansion emerges -- white stucco, red tile roof,
bougainvillea climbing the columns. Beyond it, the blue-green Caribbean stretches to the horizon.

Lucky steps out of the car. He stands for a moment, looking at the house, then past it to the ocean.

Florida is out there. Ninety miles. A lifetime away and close enough to taste.

Delgado opens the trunk and reaches for Lucky's bags. Lucky stops him.

                    LUCKY
          Carlos. You're not a bellboy. Leave it
          for the staff.

                    DELGADO
          Yes, senor.

Lucky walks toward the front door. Lansky falls into step beside him.

Lucky, quiet, to Lansky.

                    LUCKY
          I like this place.

                    LANSKY
          You haven't seen the inside yet.

                    LUCKY
          I'm not talking about the house.

They enter the mansion. Delgado stands by the car, watching them go. He straightens his jacket and looks around at the
property -- the manicured grounds, the ocean, the scale of wealth that surrounds him.

He's a Secret Police officer making a government salary. This is not his world. But for the foreseeable future, it's his
assignment.

He follows them inside.

# Scene 4 - The Cuba Plan
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - LOBBY - DAY (LATE OCTOBER 1946)

The Hotel Nacional de Cuba. Grand lobby with marble floors, mahogany columns, potted palms reaching toward the ceiling.
Bellhops in white uniforms cross the floor. Well-dressed guests linger by the bar. The quiet hum of money and privilege.

Lucky and Lansky walk through the lobby. Lansky moves like a man who owns the place -- because he partly does.

                    LANSKY
          Opened in thirty-one. Three hundred
          rooms. Churchill stayed here. Hemingway
          drinks at the bar every Thursday. Two
          restaurants, a nightclub, a casino that
          never closes.

                    LUCKY
          And you own thirty percent.

                    LANSKY
          Took me four years to get there. The
          right people, the right investments,
          the right patience.

They pause at the entrance to the casino floor. Even in daylight, it operates -- roulette wheels spinning, cards being
dealt, a low murmur of action.

                    LUCKY
          How much does it turn?

                    LANSKY
          The casino alone? Three, four million
          a year. The hotel operation on top of
          that. And that's just this building.

Lucky takes it in. The scale registers.

                    LUCKY
          I want in. What does it cost?

                    LANSKY
          For a partnership stake? Hundred and
          fifty thousand. That buys you into the
          ownership group, a permanent suite, and
          a seat at every table that matters.

                    LUCKY
          Done.

                    LANSKY
          Just like that?

                    LUCKY
          I've been sitting in Italy for six months
          with nothing to spend money on. I know
          a good investment when I see one.

A HOTEL MANAGER approaches, deferential, and shakes Lucky's hand.

                    HOTEL MANAGER
          Senor Luciano. Your suite is prepared.
          Seventh floor, ocean view. If there is
          anything you require --

                    LUCKY
          I'm sure it's perfect.

The manager bows and retreats. Lucky watches him go.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          They know who I am.

                    LANSKY
          Everyone in Havana knows who you are,
          Charlie. That's the point. In New York
          you're a convict. Here you're an investor.

EXT. ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - DAY

Bright afternoon sun. The grandstand fills with well-dressed Cubans and American tourists. Horses parade in the walking
ring. The energy of legal gambling -- electricity and optimism in the air.

Lucky and Lansky stand at the rail of the JOCKEY CLUB, an elevated private section overlooking the track. Tables behind
them, white tablecloths, waiters circulating with drinks.

                    LANSKY
          Oriental Park. Best racetrack in the
          Caribbean. The Jockey Club tables are
          where the real money moves -- private
          gambling, high stakes, invitation only.

                    LUCKY
          What's the lease?

                    LANSKY
          Fifty thousand a season for the tables.
          You run whatever you want up here --
          cards, dice, book. The racetrack takes
          its cut and looks the other way.

Lucky surveys the crowd below. Hundreds of people feeding money into the system.

                    LUCKY
          Fifty thousand to make five hundred.
          I can live with that.

They sit at one of the Jockey Club tables. A waiter brings rum without being asked.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie. The gambling is good money.
          Real money. But you and I both know
          that's not why you're here.

Lucky looks at him. The shift in tone is immediate. The charm drops. This is business.

                    LUCKY
          The China route.

                    LANSKY
          Finished. Mao's people are winning the
          civil war. Shanghai's done. Within three
          years, there won't be a gram of morphine
          coming out of the Orient.

                    LUCKY
          Which means every family on the East Coast
          is about to lose their supply.

                    LANSKY
          Not lose. Replace. That's why you're here.

Lansky leans forward. He pulls a pen from his jacket and begins drawing on a cocktail napkin. Lines, arrows, names.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          Turkey. That's where it starts. The
          poppy fields in Afyon. Raw opium, tons
          of it, and the Turks don't care where
          it goes as long as the money's right.

He draws a line south.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          From Turkey to Sicily. Your people,
          Charlie. The families in Palermo convert
          the raw opium to morphine base. Small
          labs, back roads, nobody asking questions.

Another line, west.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          Sicily to Marseille. The Corsicans.
          Best chemists in the world for this work.
          They take the morphine base and process it
          into pure heroin. Pharmaceutical grade.

                    LUCKY
          And from Marseille?

Lansky draws the final line. Across the Atlantic. To a small island.

                    LANSKY
          Havana. Right here. The product comes by
          ship to Cuban ports. And from here --

He draws lines fanning out -- north, northwest, northeast.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          Tampa. New Orleans. New York. Ninety
          miles of open water to Florida. Cuban
          customs is ours. The harbor master is
          ours. The police are ours. Nothing gets
          inspected, nothing gets stopped.

Lucky stares at the napkin. A simple drawing. An empire.

                    LUCKY
          How much?

                    LANSKY
          Conservatively? Fifty million a year.
          Within three years of full operation.

Lucky picks up the napkin. Studies it. Sets it down.

                    LUCKY
          The Corsicans -- I need to meet them.
          And the Sicilian families need to know
          the new arrangement.

                    LANSKY
          I can set it up. But Charlie -- this
          is bigger than anything we've ever done.
          This isn't New York. This is international.
          Multiple countries, multiple partners,
          shipping lanes, currencies. It works
          because Cuba is the hub. The second Cuba
          stops working --

                    LUCKY
          Cuba's not going to stop working.
          Batista's not going anywhere. Valdes
          has the government locked down. And
          we're going to make everyone in this
          country so rich they'll name a street
          after me.

Lansky allows himself a small smile.

                    LANSKY
          There's one more thing. We need to
          bring the bosses in. All of them. Costello,
          Anastasia, Bonanno, Trafficante. Everyone
          who's going to work the distribution end
          needs to hear this from you. Face to face.

                    LUCKY
          A meeting.

                    LANSKY
          A conference. Right here in Havana.
          Every family head. December, maybe.
          We lay out the whole thing -- the pipeline,
          the territories, the money. They see what
          we've built, they see the opportunity,
          and they get in line.

                    LUCKY
          And Vito?

A pause. The name hangs between them.

                    LANSKY
          Genovese has to be there. He's been
          running things in New York. If you
          cut him out, he makes trouble.

                    LUCKY
          Vito always makes trouble.

                    LANSKY
          Then let him make it where you can see it.

Lucky considers this. He picks up his rum, takes a drink, and looks out over the racetrack. Horses round the final turn.
The crowd roars.

                    LUCKY
          Set it up. All of it. The meeting, the
          contacts, the pipeline. I want everything
          in place by December.

                    LANSKY
          It'll be ready.

Lucky raises his glass. Lansky raises his.

                    LUCKY
          To Cuba.

                    LANSKY
          To the future.

They drink. Below them, a horse crosses the finish line and the crowd erupts. Lucky watches it all with the eyes of a man
who sees the entire board.

# Scene 5 - The New Home
----------------------------------------

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - MORNING (EARLY NOVEMBER 1946)

Dawn over the Caribbean. The water shifts from black to indigo to turquoise as the sun climbs. The Miramar mansion
catches the first light -- white walls turning gold, bougainvillea glowing pink and red.

SUPER: MIRAMAR, HAVANA -- EARLY NOVEMBER 1946

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - BEDROOM - MORNING

Lucky stands at his bedroom window in a silk robe, coffee in hand, watching the ocean. The room is elegant -- dark wood
furniture, tile floors, high ceilings with a slowly turning fan. His clothes are already laid out on the bed by an unseen
hand. Suits, shoes, ties -- a wardrobe for a king.

He sips his coffee. Unhurried. A man settling into a life.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - HALLWAY / KITCHEN - MORNING

Lucky walks through the mansion. STAFF move through the house -- a HOUSEKEEPER polishing silver, a COOK preparing
breakfast. They nod respectfully as he passes.

Lucky pauses in the kitchen. The cook -- an older Cuban woman -- is slicing fruit. He watches her work.

                    LUCKY
          What's your name?

                    COOK
          Elena, senor.

                    LUCKY
          Elena. That coffee this morning --
          what was it?

                    COOK
          Cuban coffee, senor. From Oriente
          province. Very strong.

                    LUCKY
          Best coffee I ever had.

Elena smiles. Lucky takes a piece of mango from the cutting board and walks on. He knows how to make people feel valued.
It's instinctive, not calculated.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - FRONT DRIVE - MORNING

Delgado waits by the car, reading a newspaper. He straightens when Lucky emerges from the house.

                    DELGADO
          Good morning, senor. Where would you
          like to go today?

                    LUCKY
          I don't know. That's the problem. I've
          been here a week and all I've seen is
          the hotel and the racetrack. Show me
          the city. The real city.

                    DELGADO
          The real city, senor?

                    LUCKY
          Not where the tourists go. Where you go.
          Where people live.

Delgado considers this. He folds the newspaper.

                    DELGADO
          I can do that.

EXT. VEDADO DISTRICT - DAY

The sedan moves through Vedado. Wider streets, grand houses, the University of Havana on a hill. Delgado drives,
narrating without being asked.

                    DELGADO
          This is Vedado. The newer part of
          Havana. The university, the best
          restaurants, the Malecon along the
          water.

                    LUCKY
          What's "Malecon"?

                    DELGADO
          The sea wall. Five miles along the
          coast. In the evening, the whole city
          comes there. Fishermen, lovers, musicians.
          It is the heart of Havana.

Lucky looks out the window. Students carrying books. An old man selling newspapers. A woman hanging laundry from a
balcony.

                    LUCKY
          It's alive, this city. You feel it.

                    DELGADO
          Havana does not sleep, senor. It only
          rests.

EXT. OLD HAVANA - DAY

Narrow colonial streets. Crumbling pastel buildings, wrought-iron balconies, cats sleeping in doorways. A world that
predates everything Lucky knows.

They walk now -- Delgado a step behind, as security protocol demands. Lucky stops at a fruit stand, examines the guavas,
buys a bag with more money than they're worth. The vendor stares at the bill.

                    LUCKY
          Your family -- they from Havana?

                    DELGADO
          My wife is from Havana. I am from
          Camaguey. In the east.

                    LUCKY
          What brought you here?

                    DELGADO
          The police academy. And then Maria.

                    LUCKY
          How old are your girls?

                    DELGADO
          Sofia is seven. Ana is five.

                    LUCKY
          Seven and five. Good ages. That's
          when they still think their father
          knows everything.

Delgado almost smiles.

                    DELGADO
          Sofia has already started to doubt it.

                    LUCKY
          They get that from their mothers.

A genuine moment between them. Delgado's guard drops, just slightly.

They walk past a small park where children play. Lucky watches them for a moment -- something wistful in his expression
that he quickly suppresses.

                    DELGADO
          Do you have children, senor?

                    LUCKY
          No. Never had the time. Or maybe never
          had the right kind of life for it.

A beat. Lucky starts walking again.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          This neighborhood -- what's it called?

                    DELGADO
          Habana Vieja. Old Havana. The original
          city. Four hundred years old.

                    LUCKY
          In New York, four hundred years gets
          you a plaque on a wall. Here it gets
          you the whole neighborhood.

                    DELGADO
          We have many plaques also.

Lucky laughs -- a real laugh, surprised out of him. Delgado allows himself a small smile.

EXT. MALECON - LATE AFTERNOON

The seawall. The light has turned golden. Waves crash against the rocks, sending spray into the air. Fishermen cast
lines. A couple leans against the wall, wrapped around each other.

Lucky and Delgado stand at the wall, looking north across the water. The wind carries salt and warmth.

                    LUCKY
          That's Florida.

                    DELGADO
          On a clear day, they say you can
          almost see it.

                    LUCKY
          Ninety miles.

Lucky stares at the horizon. The distance between himself and everything he lost. And everything he's about to build.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          You know what I like about this country,
          Carlos? Nobody's trying to put me in
          a box. In New York, in Italy -- everybody
          wants you in a box. Here, there's room
          to breathe.

Delgado says nothing. He stands beside Lucky, watching the water. A protector beside the man he protects. He doesn't
fully understand what Lucky is -- not yet. But he's beginning to sense the gravity of it.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - TERRACE - EVENING

Sunset. The sky blazes orange and pink over the ocean. Lucky sits on the terrace with a drink, alone. The mansion behind
him, the ocean before him. Between them, a man calculating his next twenty moves.

A CAR PULLS UP in the drive. Delgado gets out. He looks up at the terrace.

                    DELGADO
          Good night, senor. I will be here at
          eight tomorrow.

                    LUCKY
          Carlos.

Delgado stops.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Thank you. For today. For showing me
          the real city.

                    DELGADO
          It was my pleasure, senor.

Delgado gets back in the car and drives out through the iron gates. Lucky watches the taillights disappear down the
tree-lined road.

He turns back to the ocean. Takes a drink. The sun sinks below the horizon line.

Lucky Luciano, alone in his palace, ninety miles from everything. Building something. Becoming something.

The question is what.

# Scene 6 - November Montage (MONTAGE)
----------------------------------------

MONTAGE - LUCKY'S EMPIRE TAKES SHAPE (NOVEMBER 1946)

Cuban music rises -- a full son orchestra, brass and percussion, the heartbeat of Havana.

-- ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - DAY. Lucky sits at the Jockey Club tables, surrounded by well-dressed gamblers. He watches
the horses round the far turn, cigar in hand, utterly at ease. Money changes hands across the green felt. A CUBAN
BUSINESSMAN shakes Lucky's hand -- another contact made, another door opened.

-- CASINO NACIONAL - NIGHT. Lucky walks the casino floor with Lansky. Slot machines ring, roulette wheels spin, cards
snap against felt. VALDES appears from the crowd, guiding a nervous-looking GOVERNMENT OFFICIAL toward Lucky. Handshakes.
Smiles. Valdes slips an envelope into the official's breast pocket and pats it smooth. The man relaxes. Every door opens.

-- MIRAMAR MANSION - DAY. The mansion as command center. Lucky takes a meeting in the study -- three MEN IN SUITS spread
papers across a desk, pointing at maps. A PHONE RINGS and Lucky answers it, speaking rapid Italian. Delgado stands at the
door, watchful, a sentinel. Staff serve cafecito in small cups.

-- MIRAMAR MANSION - NIGHT. A different meeting. More men, more papers, more phone calls. The machinery of empire never
stops. Through the window, the ocean shimmers under moonlight.

-- HAVANA DOCKS - DAWN. The critical beat. Early morning fog. Cargo ships loom at anchor. Lucky stands on a wharf with
SANTO TRAFFICANTE, mid-50s, the Tampa boss already embedded in Cuba's port operations. Trafficante is all business -- he
knows these docks, knows the harbormaster, knows which ships don't get inspected. He walks Lucky through a shipping
manifest, tapping certain lines. Lucky nods. Trafficante produces a small sample -- a paper packet, white powder inside.
Lucky examines it, rubs a trace between his fingers, nods again. The packet disappears. Handshakes -- the grip of men
sealing something permanent. Delgado stands thirty feet away, by the car. He watches the meeting but hears nothing. His
face is still. But his eyes are uneasy. Something about this feels different from the racetracks and casinos. He can't
name it. He doesn't try.

-- VALDES'S OFFICE - DAY. Valdes at his desk, phone to his ear, signing documents without reading them. A stack of
PERMITS on his desk -- gaming licenses, import authorizations, building approvals. He stamps one after another. A
SECRETARY brings more. He stamps those too. Behind him, a framed portrait of President Batista watches from the wall.

-- ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - LATE AFTERNOON. New faces in the Jockey Club. AMERICAN ASSOCIATES -- Lucky's men from the
States -- take positions behind the tables. They move with an authority that displaces the old staff. DELGADO'S COUSIN,
30s, a racetrack worker in a white shirt, is stopped at a door he used to walk through. An American shakes his head. No.
The cousin stands there for a moment, disbelieving. Then he takes off his cap, looks back at the track one last time, and
walks away.

The music fades. The montage ends on:

INT. DELGADO'S CAR - EVENING

Delgado drives home alone. He passes through the working-class streets of his neighborhood -- a different Havana from the
mansions and casinos. He stops at a light. Stares at nothing.

He's heard about the cousin. He doesn't speak about it. But the weight of it sits on his face -- the first crack in the
simple story he's been telling himself about what this job is.

The light changes. He drives on.

# Scene 7 - Delgado Family Dinner
----------------------------------------

INT. DELGADO HOME - KITCHEN / DINING ROOM - NIGHT (NOVEMBER 1946)

A modest Cuban home. Clean, warm, full of life. Tile floors, painted walls, a crucifix above the doorway. The kitchen
opens directly into the dining area where a long table is set for ten.

MARIA DELGADO, late 30s, dark-haired and capable, moves between stove and table with practiced efficiency. Steam rises
from pots. The smell of roast pork and black beans fills the house.

Sofia, 7, sets plates on the table with exaggerated care. ANA, 5, follows behind, placing napkins that immediately slide
off.

                    MARIA
          Sofia, help your sister with the napkins.

                    SOFIA
          She keeps putting them upside down.

                    ANA
          There's no upside down for napkins!

                    SOFIA
          Yes there is. Papa said so.

The front door opens. Delgado enters, still in his suit from work. Both girls rush him.

                    ANA
          Papa!

                    SOFIA
          Papa, tell Ana there's an upside down
          for napkins.

Delgado scoops Ana up with one arm and puts his free hand on Sofia's head.

                    DELGADO
          There is absolutely an upside down
          for napkins. But tonight, we don't care.

He kisses Maria. A brief, comfortable kiss -- the kind that comes from years together.

                    MARIA
          You're late.

                    DELGADO
          Traffic on the Malecon.

                    MARIA
          You're always late since the new
          assignment.

                    DELGADO
          The new assignment pays for the new
          curtains you wanted.

She gives him a look -- conceding the point but not the argument.

A KNOCK at the door. Maria opens it to DELGADO'S UNCLE TOMAS, 60s, barrel-chested and weathered, accompanied by AUNT
CARMEN, and behind them RAFAEL, Delgado's cousin, 30s, the one who lost his racetrack job. Rafael is clean-shaven,
wearing his best shirt, but there's something deflated about him.

Embraces, kisses on cheeks, the overlapping warmth of extended family arriving.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Carlos! Look at you. That suit is new.

                    DELGADO
          It's not new.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          It looks new.

Maria, calling from the kitchen.

                    MARIA
          It's new.

Laughter. Delgado loosens his tie and shakes his head.

INT. DELGADO HOME - DINING TABLE - LATER

The table is full. Family passes dishes, pours wine, talks over each other. The girls eat quietly, watching the adults.
It's loud, warm, alive -- the kind of dinner that defines a family.

                    DELGADO
          Maria, this pork is perfect.

                    MARIA
          It's the same pork I always make.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Then you always make perfect pork.

                    AUNT CARMEN
          Tomas, stop flirting with your
          nephew's wife.

More laughter. Delgado leans back, content. He watches his family with genuine warmth. This is what he works for. This
table, these people, this noise.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          So, Carlos. The new position. It
          agrees with you.

                    DELGADO
          It's a good assignment.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Important man you're protecting, from
          what I hear.

                    DELGADO
          An important visitor to Cuba. That's all.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          An important visitor who's taken over
          half the racetrack.

The warmth at the table shifts. Not dramatically -- but the temperature drops a degree. Rafael looks at his plate.

                    DELGADO
          Tio, let's not --

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Rafael has been at Oriental Park for
          eight years. Eight years. And last week
          they tell him he's finished. Americans
          are taking over his section. No notice,
          no severance, nothing.

Rafael, quiet.

                    RAFAEL
          Tio, it's all right.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          It's not all right. He has a wife. He
          has rent. You know how hard it is to
          find work right now?

Delgado sets his fork down carefully.

                    DELGADO
          I'm sorry about what happened to Rafael.
          But that has nothing to do with me.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Nothing to do with you? You work for
          the man whose people fired him.

                    DELGADO
          I don't work for him. I'm assigned to
          protect him. There's a difference.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Is there?

A beat. The table is quiet. Even the girls have stopped eating.

                    AUNT CARMEN
          Tomas --

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Carlos, I'm not attacking you. I'm
          asking you. You have a relationship
          with this man. You drive him, you're
          with him every day. Talk to him. Ask
          him to look into Rafael's situation.
          One word from you --

                    DELGADO
          I'm not going to do that.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          Why not?

                    DELGADO
          Because I am a police officer. I don't
          ask favors from the people I'm assigned
          to protect. That's not how it works.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          That is exactly how it works, Carlos.
          That is how everything in this country
          works. You know someone, you ask. That's
          Cuba.

                    DELGADO
          Not for me.

                    RAFAEL
          Carlos, forget it. I'll find something
          else. I don't want this to --

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          You shouldn't have to find something
          else. You had a job. These Americans
          come in and take what they want because
          they have money and connections. And
          your cousin has connections now.
          (to Delgado)
          Whether you like it or not.

Delgado pushes back from the table. Not violently -- but firmly. Everyone feels it.

                    DELGADO
          I am not connected. I am not part of
          this man's business. I carry a badge.
          I carry a government identification
          card. I report to the Secret Police
          command. That is what I am.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          And the new suit? And the new curtains
          Maria mentioned? Where does that money
          come from?

                    MARIA
          Tomas, that's enough.

                    UNCLE TOMAS
          It comes from the assignment. The
          assignment to protect a man whose people
          are taking jobs from your family.

Silence. Delgado's jaw tightens. He looks at Rafael, who won't meet his eyes. At Maria, whose expression holds both
sympathy and the quiet acknowledgment that Tomas isn't entirely wrong. At his daughters, who are watching with the wide,
uncertain eyes of children who feel tension they don't understand.

                    DELGADO
          I think dinner is over.

                    MARIA
          Carlos --

                    DELGADO
          Tomas, Carmen -- thank you for coming.
          Rafael, I'm sorry about the job. I
          genuinely am.

He stands and walks out of the room. The family sits in the aftermath. Maria closes her eyes briefly, then begins
clearing plates, because someone has to.

INT. DELGADO HOME - BACK PORCH - NIGHT

Small concrete porch. A clothesline, a single chair, the sounds of the neighborhood at night -- a radio playing
somewhere, a dog barking, distant traffic.

Delgado sits in the chair, smoking. He rarely smokes -- Maria doesn't like it. But tonight he needs something in his
hands.

The door opens. Maria comes out. She leans against the doorframe.

                    MARIA
          They've gone home.

Delgado nods.

                    MARIA (CONT'D)
          You know Tomas. He speaks before
          he thinks.

                    DELGADO
          He wasn't wrong.

Maria comes closer. She sits on the arm of his chair.

                    MARIA
          You're a good man, Carlos. And
          you're a good policeman.

                    DELGADO
          Those two things are getting harder
          to be at the same time.

She puts her hand on the back of his neck. They sit together in the dark.

                    MARIA
          The girls are asking why Uncle Tomas
          was upset.

                    DELGADO
          What did you tell them?

                    MARIA
          That adults sometimes disagree about
          things that aren't simple.

Delgado stubs out the cigarette.

                    DELGADO
          That's the best answer anyone's given
          tonight.

Maria goes back inside. Through the window, Delgado can see her putting the girls to bed -- tucking covers, smoothing
hair.

He sits alone. The question from dinner hangs in the air: where does the duty end and the compromise begin? He doesn't
have an answer. The cigarette smoke drifts into the dark.

# Scene 8 - Conference Preparations
----------------------------------------

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - STUDY - DAY (LATE NOVEMBER 1946)

SUPER: LATE NOVEMBER 1946

The study has been transformed into a war room. A large table dominates the center, covered with papers -- guest lists,
hotel floor plans, seating charts. Lucky stands at the head, jacket off, sleeves rolled to the forearm. Lansky sits with
a leather notebook, pen working steadily.

                    LUCKY
          How many confirmed?

                    LANSKY
          Twenty-two as of yesterday. Costello's
          in. Anastasia's in. Bonanno, Profaci,
          Trafficante, the Fischetti brothers.
          Moretti, Adonis, Magliocco. Everybody
          who matters on the East Coast.

                    LUCKY
          West Coast?

                    LANSKY
          Dragna from L.A. is coming. Jack
          from Detroit. Dalitz might send
          someone.

                    LUCKY
          And Vito?

                    LANSKY
          Confirmed. He wouldn't miss it. Can't
          afford to.

Lucky nods. He picks up the guest list, scans it.

                    LUCKY
          Twenty bosses and their people. That's
          fifty, sixty men flying into Havana in
          one week. Somebody's going to notice.

                    LANSKY
          That's why we need the cover. I've been
          talking to the Fischetti brothers about
          entertainment. They can bring Sinatra.

                    LUCKY
          Frank Sinatra?

                    LANSKY
          He owes the Fischettis. They got him
          out of his contract with Tommy Dorsey.
          He'll come, he'll perform, and every
          newspaper in Cuba will report on a
          celebrity party at the Hotel Nacional.
          Nobody's looking at the guest list
          behind the guest list.

                    LUCKY
          Sinatra. That's good. That's very good.

Lucky sits down. He pulls the hotel floor plan closer.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          The Nacional ballroom -- how big?

                    LANSKY
          Seats three hundred for dinner. We'll
          use the private dining rooms for the
          real meetings. The ballroom for the
          opening night -- dinner, entertainment,
          the envelope ceremony.

                    LUCKY
          The envelopes. How much are we talking?

                    LANSKY
          Every boss brings a tribute. Two hundred
          thousand minimum in cash gifts. The
          Fischettis are also transporting a
          suitcase with two million from the
          New York operation.

                    LUCKY
          Two million in a suitcase.

                    LANSKY
          Dollars, not pesos. Welcome-home money.
          They want you to know they're behind
          you.

Lucky absorbs this. Two million dollars. The loyalty of twenty crime families, expressed in currency.

                    LUCKY
          The agenda. Three days?

                    LANSKY
          Four. December twenty-second through
          the twenty-fifth. Opening night
          the twenty-second -- dinner, Sinatra,
          the tributes. Twenty-third and twenty-
          fourth, working sessions. Heroin
          territories, the pipeline, Cuba
          operations, the Siegel situation.
          Christmas Day, we close.

                    LUCKY
          Siegel.

                    LANSKY
          That has to be addressed. The
          Flamingo's budget went from one
          million to six. Half the bosses
          think he's skimming. They want
          his head.

                    LUCKY
          Ben's your friend.

                    LANSKY
          He's everyone's friend until he
          costs them money. I'll handle it.
          Give him until the hotel opens
          in December -- if the Flamingo
          turns a profit, he keeps breathing.

                    LUCKY
          And if it doesn't?

Lansky doesn't answer. He doesn't have to.

INT. MINISTER VALDES'S OFFICE - DAY

A government office that radiates power -- dark wood, Cuban flag, portrait of Batista. Valdes sits behind an enormous
desk, phone to his ear.

Valdes, into phone.

                    VALDES
          The Hotel Nacional, the third week
          of December. Full booking. Private
          event, closed to the public for four
          days. The cover is a holiday
          celebration with American entertainers.

He listens.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          The President's office will handle
          the security clearances. I need a
          letter from the Interior Ministry
          approving the temporary closure.

He listens again.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          No, there will be no press access.
          No exceptions.
          (beat)
          Because I said so. And because the
          President says so. Is there a third
          reason you require?

He hangs up. Picks up the next phone. Dials.

Valdes (cont'd), into phone.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          Customs office, please.
          (waits)
          This is Minister Valdes. I need a
          standing order for the week of
          December twentieth. Private aircraft
          arriving from the United States -- ten,
          perhaps fifteen planes. Diplomatic
          courtesy processing. No inspections,
          no manifests filed, no passenger
          records kept.

He makes a note in his ledger. Another call. Another arrangement. The machinery of a government turned to the service of
organized crime, operated by a single man who makes it all look effortless.

INT. DELGADO'S CAR / EXT. HOTEL NACIONAL - DAY

Delgado pulls up to the Hotel Nacional. He's in uniform today -- more formal than usual. He enters the lobby and is met
by a SENIOR OFFICER from the Secret Police, CAPTAIN HERRERA, 50s, a hard man with a clipped mustache.

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          Delgado. Thank you for coming.

                    DELGADO
          Sir. Your message said increased
          security requirements.

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          Correct. We have a private event at
          the Nacional in three weeks. Four days,
          December twenty-second through twenty-
          fifth. American VIPs. Very important
          guests of the Cuban government.

                    DELGADO
          How many guests?

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          Fifty to sixty. They will require full
          security -- perimeter, lobby, corridors,
          the ballroom and private dining rooms.
          Discreet presence. These men value
          their privacy.

                    DELGADO
          What kind of event is this, sir?

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          A holiday celebration. Entertainment,
          dinners. The cover story is Frank
          Sinatra performing.

                    DELGADO
          The cover story?

Herrera gives him a flat look.

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          The event story. For the press. You
          understand.

                    DELGADO
          I understand, sir.

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          You will coordinate hotel security.
          Additional agents will be assigned.
          Your familiarity with Senor Luciano
          and his associates makes you the
          logical choice.

                    DELGADO
          Yes, sir.

                    CAPTAIN HERRERA
          One more thing. Discretion is not
          a suggestion. Whatever you see during
          this event, whatever you hear -- it
          stays in the hotel. This comes from
          Minister Valdes personally. And what
          comes from Valdes comes from the
          President.

                    DELGADO
          Understood.

Herrera leaves. Delgado stands in the Hotel Nacional lobby, looking around at the grand space. Bellhops cross the marble
floor. Tourists drink at the bar. A pianist plays something soft.

In three weeks, this elegant hotel will host every major criminal in America. And Delgado will be standing at the door.

He doesn't know the scale of what's coming. But the phrase "cover story" echoes in his mind. A police officer who's been
told not to report what he sees. Another line crossed.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - TERRACE - EVENING

Lucky and Lansky on the terrace. Cigars, rum, the ocean at dusk. The planning is done. The invitations are sent. The
machinery is in motion.

                    LANSKY
          Three weeks. Everything's in place.

                    LUCKY
          You know the last time all the bosses
          were in one room? Atlantic City. Thirty-
          one. I called that meeting too.

                    LANSKY
          You were thirty-four years old.

                    LUCKY
          I was young and I was stupid and I
          thought I could organize the whole
          thing into something clean. A
          commission, rules, territories.
          Business instead of war.

                    LANSKY
          It worked.

                    LUCKY
          For fifteen years. Then they put me
          in prison and Vito started picking it
          apart. Now I'm doing it again. From
          further away, with higher stakes, and
          Vito sitting at the same table.

                    LANSKY
          You worried about him?

                    LUCKY
          I'm worried about what he does if I
          don't worry about him.

Lansky nods. He understands. The conference isn't just about the heroin pipeline or the gambling territories. It's about
control. About proving that exile didn't diminish Lucky Luciano. About making twenty bosses look him in the eye and say
yes.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          December twenty-second. Every family
          in America. The biggest meeting in the
          history of this thing.

He raises his glass.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Let's make sure it's not the last.

They drink. The sun drops below the horizon. Havana glitters in the gathering dark.

# Scene 9 - The Hunt Begins
----------------------------------------

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - CORRIDOR - DAY (DECEMBER 1946)

SUPER: FEDERAL BUREAU OF NARCOTICS -- WASHINGTON, D.C. -- DECEMBER 1946

Government gray. Fluorescent lights hum over linoleum floors. Filing cabinets line the walls. AGENTS move through the
corridor with folders and purpose. The institutional machinery of federal law enforcement -- unglamorous, relentless.

JACK BRENNAN walks the corridor, summoned. He wears a dark suit, white shirt, no tie -- a man pulled from his desk
without warning. He carries nothing but the knot in his jaw.

A SECRETARY at a desk outside an office door looks up.

                    SECRETARY
          Agent Brennan? The Director is
          expecting you. Go right in.

INT. DIRECTOR HARTLEY'S OFFICE - CONTINUOUS

The office of a powerful man who doesn't need to advertise it. Government-issue desk, American flag in the corner, framed
photographs with politicians on the wall. No personal effects. No warmth.

DIRECTOR WILLIAM HARTLEY sits behind his desk, reading a file. He doesn't look up when Brennan enters. A manila folder
lies open before him, next to two photographs and a decoded cable.

                    HARTLEY
          Close the door.

Brennan closes it. Stands.

                    HARTLEY (CONT'D)
          Sit down, Jack.

Brennan sits. Hartley continues reading for a long moment. Then he closes the folder and looks up. His expression is
controlled, but there's something in his eyes -- not alarm, not exactly excitement. Calculation.

                    HARTLEY (CONT'D)
          How long has it been since we stood
          on those courthouse steps?

                    BRENNAN
          Ten months. February.

                    HARTLEY
          Ten months. And in those ten months,
          what did we believe about Lucky Luciano?

                    BRENNAN
          That he was in Italy. Retired.
          Neutralized.

Hartley slides one of the photographs across the desk. Brennan picks it up.

It's a surveillance photo -- grainy, taken from a distance. Lucky Luciano at a racetrack, cigar in hand, surrounded by
well-dressed men. Behind him, palm trees and Caribbean sky.

                    BRENNAN
          Where is this?

                    HARTLEY
          Havana.

Brennan stares at the photo. His expression doesn't change, but something behind his eyes goes very still.

                    BRENNAN
          When?

                    HARTLEY
          Last month. He arrived October twenty-
          ninth. He's been there two months. He's
          invested in the Hotel Nacional, leased
          gambling tables at the racetrack, and
          he's operating freely with full Cuban
          government protection.

Hartley slides the second photograph. A wider shot -- Lucky with Lansky outside the Hotel Nacional, shaking hands with a
man in a guayabera. Minister Valdes.

                    BRENNAN
          That's Meyer Lansky with him.

                    HARTLEY
          Lansky's been in Cuba for years.
          He owns thirty percent of the Hotel
          Nacional. But that's not why I called
          you in.

Hartley opens the decoded cable and places it in front of Brennan.

                    HARTLEY (CONT'D)
          Intelligence came in this morning.
          Every major crime boss in America is
          booking travel to Havana for the third
          week of December. Costello, Genovese,
          Anastasia, Bonanno, Trafficante --
          the entire leadership of organized
          crime in the United States, converging
          on one city.

                    BRENNAN
          A summit.

                    HARTLEY
          More than a summit. This looks like a
          full conference. Twenty-plus bosses.
          The Hotel Nacional has been booked for
          a private event -- cover story is a
          celebrity party with Frank Sinatra.

Brennan sets the cable down. He leans back in his chair.

                    BRENNAN
          He's been there two months and we
          didn't know.

                    HARTLEY
          The Cubans aren't sharing intelligence.
          Batista's government is compromised --
          the entire apparatus is on Luciano's
          payroll. We had no assets in Havana
          watching for this.

                    BRENNAN
          We deported him. We put him on a ship
          and we sent him across the ocean and
          we called it a victory.

                    HARTLEY
          That was the policy at the time.

                    BRENNAN
          The policy was wrong.

A beat. Hartley's expression doesn't change. He lets Brennan's anger land, then steers past it.

                    HARTLEY
          I didn't call you in to relitigate
          February. I called you in because
          I have an assignment and you're the
          right man for it.

                    BRENNAN
          What assignment?

                    HARTLEY
          Havana. I need an agent on the ground
          at the Hotel Nacional before this
          conference begins. Undercover -- tourist
          cover, businessman, whatever works.
          You check into the hotel, you recruit
          informants among the staff, you identify
          every boss who walks through those doors,
          and you document everything.

                    BRENNAN
          A surveillance operation.

                    HARTLEY
          Intelligence gathering. Building a case.
          We need evidence of what Luciano is doing
          in Cuba -- specifically, anything connecting
          him to narcotics trafficking. The deportation
          was immigration enforcement. This time, I
          want a narcotics case. Something Washington
          can use as leverage.

                    BRENNAN
          Leverage for what?

                    HARTLEY
          To make Cuba choose between Luciano
          and the United States.

Brennan absorbs this. He looks at the photographs again. Lucky at the racetrack. Lucky at the Hotel Nacional. A man who
was supposed to be finished, operating ninety miles from Florida like a king in his court.

                    BRENNAN
          Why me?

Hartley meets his eyes. Holds them.

                    HARTLEY
          Because you told me on those courthouse
          steps that it wasn't over. Because you
          were right. And because I know what
          Lucky Luciano did to your family.

A long beat. Brennan's jaw tightens. The mention of his brother -- the unspoken name between them -- changes the
temperature in the room.

                    BRENNAN
          That's not why I'd take the assignment.

                    HARTLEY
          I know. But it's why you'll be good
          at it.

Silence. The clock on the wall ticks. Traffic sounds filter through the window.

                    BRENNAN
          What's my lifeline?

                    HARTLEY
          Me. Phone calls and telegrams. I'm
          your handler, your authority, your
          only contact in Washington. You report
          to me, I give you guidance. Nobody else
          at the Bureau knows the details of this
          operation.

                    BRENNAN
          And if things go wrong in Havana?

                    HARTLEY
          Then you use your judgment and you
          call me. That's what I'm here for.

Brennan stands. He looks at the photographs one more time.

                    BRENNAN
          When do I leave?

                    HARTLEY
          Today. There's a flight to Miami at
          four. From Miami, Pan American to
          Havana. You'll be at the Nacional
          by tomorrow night.

                    BRENNAN
          That's fast.

                    HARTLEY
          The conference starts in days. We're
          already behind.

Brennan nods. He picks up the photographs and the cable, then stops.

                    BRENNAN
          Sir. The heroin that killed my brother
          came through Luciano's network. Every
          agent in this building knows that. I
          watched him walk out of that courthouse
          and I told you it wasn't over.

                    HARTLEY
          I remember.

                    BRENNAN
          It wasn't over. I'm going to prove it.

                    HARTLEY
          That's exactly what I need you to do.

Brennan takes the file and walks to the door. He pauses with his hand on the knob.

                    BRENNAN
          The conference intelligence -- the
          cable. Where did it come from?

A flicker across Hartley's face. Something guarded, almost imperceptible.

                    HARTLEY
          Informant network. Standard channels.
          Why?

                    BRENNAN
          Just wondering how we missed him for
          two months and then suddenly know
          everything.

                    HARTLEY
          That's how intelligence works. You're
          blind until you're not. Go catch your
          plane.

Brennan nods and exits.

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - CORRIDOR - CONTINUOUS

Brennan walks back down the corridor with the file under his arm. His stride is different now -- longer, faster, a man
with direction.

He passes the same filing cabinets, the same fluorescent lights, the same institutional gray. But everything looks
different when you're leaving it behind.

EXT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - DAY

Brennan emerges into the December cold. Washington, D.C. -- monumental, bureaucratic, a world of marble and procedure.

He stands on the steps for a moment, breath steaming in the air. He looks south -- instinctively, toward Florida, toward
Cuba, toward the man he's been waiting ten months to hunt.

Then he walks to his car, gets in, and drives.

The hunt begins.

SMASH CUT TO BLACK.

                    END OF ACT ONE

# Scene 10 - Arrival and Opening Night (MONTAGE)
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - LOBBY - DAY (DECEMBER 20, 1946)

A magnificent colonial lobby. Marble floors, palm fronds, ceiling fans
turning slow. Porters in white jackets attend to wealthy guests.

JACK BRENNAN, late 30s, hard-jawed, wearing a linen suit that doesn't
quite fit the surroundings, approaches the front desk with a leather
suitcase. His eyes sweep the room -- cataloging exits, sightlines, the
elevator bank.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          I checked into the Hotel Nacional on
          December twentieth under the name
          James Burke, textile buyer from
          Philadelphia. Room 412, fourth floor.
          The bosses were on the eighth. Three
          hundred rooms in that hotel, and these
          men had spent their whole lives
          believing Cuba was untouchable. They
          weren't looking for surveillance.
          They weren't looking at me. They were
          looking at who was coming.

He signs the register. Takes his key. His gaze lingers on the grand
staircase, the balcony overlooking the lobby.

He turns from the desk and nearly collides with a man crossing the
lobby. Dark overcoat, hat tilted at a precise angle, two men flanking
him. LUCKY LUCIANO walks past Brennan close enough to touch -- close
enough that Brennan catches the scent of his cologne.

Lucky doesn't look at him. Why would he? A textile buyer from
Philadelphia is invisible.

Brennan stands frozen. Watching Lucky cross the lobby and disappear
into the elevator with Lansky. The doors close. Gone.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          That was the first time I saw him in
          person since the courthouse steps.
          Close enough to reach out and touch
          the man I blamed for my brother's
          death. He didn't see me. He never
          saw me. That was the whole point.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - SERVICE CORRIDOR - DAY

Brennan walks alongside ROSA, 40s, the hotel telephone operator, in a
narrow service hallway. He speaks low, his hand pressing a folded bill
into her palm.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Rosa at the switchboard. Every call
          in and out of the hotel went through
          her. Twenty dollars a week bought me
          a list of every number dialed from
          the eighth floor.

Rosa tucks the money into her apron without breaking stride.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - ELEVATOR - DAY

Brennan steps into the elevator. ERNESTO, 20s, the attendant, pulls
the brass gate shut. Brennan palms him a bill with a handshake.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Ernesto ran the elevator. He knew
          every face, every floor, who visited
          whom and when. Cost me ten dollars
          and a promise I'd get his cousin a
          visa. I lied about the visa.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BAR - EVENING

Amber light. A mahogany bar with Art Deco mirrors. CARLOS DELGADO,
early 40s, sits alone with a glass of rum, his posture rigid, his suit
too well-pressed for a tourist. He watches the room with trained eyes.

Brennan settles onto a stool two seats away. Orders a bourbon. Their
eyes meet in the mirror behind the bar. A beat of mutual assessment.

Delgado clocks Brennan's watchfulness. Brennan clocks Delgado's
bearing -- law enforcement, not mob. Neither speaks. Delgado returns
to his drink. Brennan does the same.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          There was a man at the bar I couldn't
          place. Not a guest. Not staff. He had
          the look of someone watching the same
          things I was watching. I didn't know
          his name yet. I'd learn it soon enough.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - NIGHT

Brennan sits on the bed, phone receiver pressed to his ear. A notepad
on his knee, hotel blueprints spread on the bedspread.

                    BRENNAN
          I'm in. Room 412. The hotel's set up
          on a grid -- eighth floor reserved
          for VIPs, ballroom on the ground
          level, service access through the
          basement. I've got eyes on the
          switchboard and the elevator.

INTERCUT WITH:

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - HARTLEY'S OFFICE - NIGHT

DIRECTOR WILLIAM HARTLEY, late 50s, silver-haired, sits behind a
massive desk. An American flag behind him. He holds the phone with
practiced ease.

                    HARTLEY
          Good. Who's arrived?

                    BRENNAN
          Nobody yet. But the eighth floor's
          been cleared and locked. They're
          expecting a large party. Very large.

                    HARTLEY
          How large?

                    BRENNAN
          They've reserved the entire floor.
          Twenty-plus rooms. Something's coming,
          Director. Something big.

Hartley, a beat -- he already knows.

                    HARTLEY
          Keep your eyes open, Jack. Names and
          faces. Document everything. I want to
          know who walks through that door.

Brennan hangs up. Stares at the phone.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Hartley told me to watch. So I
          watched.

EXT. HOTEL NACIONAL - ENTRANCE - DAY (DECEMBER 21, 1946)

A black Cadillac pulls to the curb. A DOORMAN opens the rear door.

FRANK COSTELLO steps out -- immaculate dark suit, silk tie, fedora
tilted at a precise angle. He adjusts his cuffs and walks inside like
he owns the building.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          December twenty-first. They started
          coming.

EXT. HOTEL NACIONAL - ENTRANCE - CONTINUOUS

Another car. VITO GENOVESE emerges -- stocky, coiled, scanning the
surroundings with cold, appraising eyes before he moves.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Costello. Genovese.

EXT. HOTEL NACIONAL - ENTRANCE - CONTINUOUS

ALBERT ANASTASIA climbs out of a sedan -- thick neck, heavy shoulders,
a face that discourages conversation. He doesn't look around. He
doesn't need to.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Anastasia.

MONTAGE - ARRIVALS (DECEMBER 21-22)

-- JOE BONANNO arriving with a leather briefcase.
-- SANTO TRAFFICANTE stepping from a taxi, squinting in the sun.
-- THE FISCHETTI BROTHERS, laughing, carrying luggage.
-- JOE ADONIS adjusting his hat.
-- WILLIE MORETTI greeting another boss with a bear hug.

Each arrival, Brennan watches from behind a newspaper in the lobby, or
from a second-floor balcony, or through the window of the bar. A
notebook in his lap, names written in a careful hand.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Bonanno. Trafficante. The Fischetti
          boys. Adonis. Moretti. Twenty bosses
          from every territory in America,
          checking into the same hotel in
          Havana over forty-eight hours. If you
          dropped a bomb on the Nacional that
          week, organized crime in America
          would've been finished. Nobody
          dropped a bomb.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BALLROOM - NIGHT (DECEMBER 22, 1946)

The ballroom blazes with light. Crystal chandeliers. Round dinner
tables draped in white linen, set with silver and flowers. A BAND
plays Cuban jazz. WOMEN in evening gowns and MEN in tuxedos fill the
room -- the cream of American organized crime, dressed for a Christmas
party.

At a microphone on the low stage, FRANK SINATRA, 31, lean and
electric, launches into a song. His voice fills the room. The
audience applauds.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Opening night. The cover story was a
          Christmas party hosted by the
          Fischettis, with entertainment
          provided by Frank Sinatra himself.
          And there he was -- the Voice --
          singing for a room full of men who'd
          ordered more killings than the
          Pacific theater.

Sinatra croons. The bosses and their wives sway. LUCKY LUCIANO sits
at the head table with MEYER LANSKY beside him. Lucky is relaxed,
regal -- a king at his own banquet. Lansky leans over, murmurs
something. Lucky smiles.

Genovese watches from a nearby table, his expression controlled,
unreadable.

Anastasia laughs loudly at something Costello says, his meaty hand
slapping the table.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BALLROOM / CORRIDOR - NIGHT

The Fischetti Brothers escort a MAN carrying a heavy SUITCASE through
a side door into a private room off the ballroom. The door closes.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - PRIVATE ROOM - CONTINUOUS

The suitcase lands on a table. Clicks open. Stacks of cash -- neat,
banded, filling the case. Two million dollars.

Lucky looks at it. Nods. Lansky is already counting.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Christmas presents. Envelopes with
          two hundred thousand or more passed
          hand to hand like greeting cards. And
          the main event -- a suitcase with two
          million in cash delivered by the
          Fischettis. I didn't see the money.
          But I knew it was there. Rosa heard
          the calls. Ernesto saw the suitcase.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BALLROOM - NIGHT

Back in the ballroom, bosses embrace and exchange envelopes -- thick,
white, sealed. Handshakes linger. Smiles that mean business.

Lucky stands, raises his glass. The room quiets. Twenty of the most
powerful criminals in the Western Hemisphere look at one man.

                    LUCKY
          Salute.

They drink. Sinatra starts another song.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - NIGHT

Brennan alone. A single lamp. He writes in his notebook, transferring
scribbled observations into organized notes. Names. Room numbers.
Connections drawn with lines.

He stops writing. Sets down his pen. Looks at the wall.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Every major criminal in America. In
          one room. In a country where none of
          this is illegal. I was one man with a
          notebook and a telephone operator.
          And I was all the United States
          government had in that building.

He picks up the pen. Goes back to writing.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          So I kept writing.

# Scene 11 - The Inner Circle
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - PRIVATE SUITE - NIGHT (DECEMBER 22, 1946)

A corner suite, high ceilings, French doors open to a balcony where
the sound of the ocean drifts in. Cigar smoke hangs in the air. A
bottle of Scotch and four glasses on a side table.

LUCKY LUCIANO sits in a leather armchair, jacket off, shirtsleeves
rolled. He looks comfortable. He looks like a man in his own house.

MEYER LANSKY sits on the sofa to Lucky's right, legs crossed, a small
leather notebook in his hand. His posture is relaxed but his eyes miss
nothing.

ALBERT ANASTASIA stands near the balcony doors, arms folded across his
broad chest. He hasn't sat down. He doesn't intend to.

VITO GENOVESE occupies a chair across from Lucky -- slightly apart
from the other three, as if the furniture itself has arranged the
allegiances.

A beat of silence. Lucky lights a cigarette.

                    LUCKY
          So. Everybody's here. Everybody's
          happy. Christmas in Havana.

                    LANSKY
          Twenty-two confirmed. Trafficante
          brought two underbosses. The
          Fischettis are handling Sinatra.
          We're set.

                    LUCKY
          Good. Tomorrow we put it to the full
          room. But tonight -- the four of us
          -- I want to lay this out plain.

He leans forward.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Cuba works. It's ninety miles from
          Florida. Batista takes his cut and
          looks the other way. I've got the
          casinos, the racetrack, the airline.
          The pipeline from France is being
          built as we speak. Meyer?

                    LANSKY
          Hotel Nacional: profitable since
          September. The racetrack tables are
          pulling fifty thousand a season just
          on the lease. The airline starts
          Havana-Key West flights in the spring.
          As for the supply routes --

He opens his notebook, runs a finger down a column of figures.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          Turkey and Lebanon through Sicily,
          processed in Marseille, shipped here.
          From Havana, we move it through Tampa,
          New Orleans, New York. It's clean. It's
          fast. And it's outside American
          jurisdiction.

                    LUCKY
          That's the point. I run it from here.
          Same as running it from New York,
          except nobody can touch me.

A pause. Genovese shifts in his chair.

                    GENOVESE
          Nobody can touch you.

                    LUCKY
          That's what I said.

                    GENOVESE
          It's a nice idea, Charlie. Beautiful
          island. Beautiful setup. But I got a
          question. You don't mind?

                    LUCKY
          Ask.

                    GENOVESE
          The families back home. They got
          problems every day. Somebody muscles
          in on a territory. Somebody skims.
          Somebody needs to be disciplined.
          These things need decisions fast.
          Same day. Same hour. How does that
          work when the boss is sitting on a
          beach in Cuba?

                    LUCKY
          I got a telephone, Vito.

                    GENOVESE
          A telephone. Sure. But there's
          things a telephone can't do. There's
          a weight to being in the room. The
          bosses back home -- they need to look
          a man in the eye when he gives an
          order. They need to know he's there.
          An order from ninety miles away...

He shrugs, lets the implication hang.

                    GENOVESE (CONT'D)
          It's different.

Anastasia uncrosses his arms. His jaw tightens.

                    ANASTASIA
          What's different about it?

                    GENOVESE
          I'm asking a question, Albert.

                    ANASTASIA
          It don't sound like a question. It
          sounds like you're saying the boss
          can't do his job from here.

                    GENOVESE
          I'm saying the families might have
          concerns. Legitimate concerns. I'm
          raising them because somebody should.

                    ANASTASIA
          Somebody. Yeah. Funny how that
          somebody is always you.

The room tenses. Anastasia takes a step forward, his eyes locked on
Genovese. Lucky raises a hand -- just slightly, palm down. The
gesture is small but the authority behind it fills the room.

                    LUCKY
          Sit down, Albert.

                    ANASTASIA
          I'm fine standing.

                    LUCKY
          I know you are. Sit down anyway.

A beat. Anastasia pulls a chair over and sits. He doesn't take his
eyes off Genovese.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Vito. You got concerns, you bring
          them to me. That's what tonight is
          for. But let me be clear about
          something.

He takes a slow pull on his cigarette.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          I built this thing. Not from behind a
          desk -- from a cell in Dannemora,
          from a ship crossing the Atlantic,
          from a house in Sicily where I didn't
          know anybody on the street. I built
          it from further away than Cuba. And
          it worked. You know why?

Genovese doesn't answer.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Because the men I work with trust me.
          And because the men who don't trust
          me are smart enough to be afraid. Now.
          You have anything else you want to
          raise before tomorrow?

Genovese, a beat, then.

                    GENOVESE
          No. That covers it.

                    LUCKY
          Good.

He stubs out the cigarette. Stands. The meeting is over because Lucky
says it's over.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Tomorrow we put it to the room.
          Everybody votes, everybody agrees,
          and we move forward. Together.

He says the last word looking directly at Genovese.

Genovese rises, buttons his jacket, and leaves without another word.
Anastasia watches him go with undisguised contempt.

The door closes. A beat.

                    ANASTASIA
          He's a snake, Charlie. You know that.

                    LUCKY
          I know what he is.

                    ANASTASIA
          Then why do you let him talk?

                    LUCKY
          Because I'd rather hear what he's
          thinking than wonder about it.

Lansky closes his notebook.

                    LANSKY
          He's not going to stop.

                    LUCKY
          No. He's not.

                    LANSKY
          So what do we do?

Lucky walks to the balcony doors. Looks out at the dark ocean. The
sound of waves.

                    LUCKY
          We let him make his play tomorrow. In
          front of everybody. Then we bury him
          with a vote. A man's ambition ain't
          dangerous until he's got support. Let's
          make sure he doesn't have any.

He turns back to face them.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Get some sleep. Tomorrow's a long day.

Anastasia stands, nods to Lucky, and leaves. Lansky lingers a moment.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie.

                    LUCKY
          Yeah.

                    LANSKY
          He won't forget tomorrow. Win or lose.
          He won't forget.

                    LUCKY
          I know.

Lansky leaves. Lucky stands alone in the suite. He pours himself a
Scotch. Drinks it looking out at the ocean.

The confidence is still there. But something behind his eyes has
shifted -- the first acknowledgment that his throne has a crack in it.

# Scene 12 - The Power Struggle
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - CONFERENCE ROOM - DAY (DECEMBER 23, 1946)

A large, elegant room. Heavy curtains drawn against the Caribbean sun.
A long mahogany conference table surrounded by chairs, every seat
taken. Twenty-plus men in suits -- the complete leadership of the
American Mafia. Ashtrays. Water glasses. Cigar smoke drifting under
crystal chandeliers.

LUCKY LUCIANO sits at the head of the table. To his right, MEYER
LANSKY. To his left, FRANK COSTELLO. ALBERT ANASTASIA sits midway
down, his big frame barely contained by his chair.

VITO GENOVESE sits across from Anastasia, his posture relaxed, his
eyes sharp.

The other BOSSES fill the remaining seats: BONANNO, TRAFFICANTE,
ADONIS, MORETTI, the FISCHETTIS, and more. Guards posted outside the
closed doors.

Lucky surveys the room. Lets the silence settle. Then:

                         LUCKY
              Gentlemen. Thank you for coming to
              Havana. I know it's Christmas week
              and you got families at home. I
              appreciate the trip.

Nods around the table.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I'm not gonna waste your time. You
              know why we're here. The last two
              years, business has been strong.
              Territories are settled. The families
              are earning. But the world is
              changing, and we need to change with
              it.

He stands. Moves to a position where the entire room can see him.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              China is finished. The war, the
              Communists -- that supply line is
              dead inside of three years. We need a
              new route. I've built one. And I've
              built it from here.

He gestures at the room, at Havana beyond the walls.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Cuba. Ninety miles from Florida.
              Government that works with us. Ports,
              airfields, casinos already generating
              revenue. This is our new base of
              operations. And from here, I can lead
              this thing the same as I always have.

A pause. The room absorbs it.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I'm proposing we formalize what we
              all know: one man at the top. A Boss
              of All Bosses. Someone the families
              can look to when there's a dispute,
              when territories need settling, when
              decisions need making. That man is me.

He looks around the table. Some bosses nod. Others sit impassive.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I'm asking for a vote. Anybody got
              something to say before we do this?

A beat. Then Genovese leans forward.

                         GENOVESE
              I got something to say.

The room shifts. Eyes move to Genovese.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              Charlie, nobody here doubts what
              you've done. You built this thing
              from nothing. We all owe you. Every
              man at this table knows it.

                         LUCKY
              But.

                         GENOVESE
              But you're not in New York. You're
              not in Chicago. You're not in New
              Orleans or Miami or anywhere in the
              United States. You're in Cuba.

He looks around the room, making eye contact with individual bosses.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              I respect the setup here. I do.
              Beautiful island, beautiful operation.
              But running twenty families from an
              island -- that's something different.
              The problems we deal with every day --
              union trouble, cops, the feds,
              territory fights -- they don't wait
              for a phone call from Havana. They
              need somebody on the ground. Somebody
              who can walk into a room and handle
              things face to face.

Some bosses shift uncomfortably. A few nod.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              I'm not challenging you, Charlie. I'm
              asking a question on behalf of every
              man in this room. Can a boss in exile
              really run things the way they need
              to be run?

                         COSTELLO
              Charlie ran things from a prison cell
              in Dannemora for ten years, Vito.
              Business didn't exactly collapse.

                         GENOVESE
              Dannemora was in New York. Still
              American soil. A man in a cell up the
              river is different than a man across
              an ocean.

                         COSTELLO
              It's not an ocean. It's the Florida
              Strait.

                         GENOVESE
              It's foreign soil, Frank. Foreign
              government. Foreign jurisdiction.
              When the boss is that far away and
              something goes wrong --

                         ANASTASIA
              What's gonna go wrong?

Genovese turns to Anastasia.

                         GENOVESE
              I'm making a point, Albert.

                         ANASTASIA
              So am I. What's gonna go wrong? You
              tell me. Because the way I see it,
              the only problem in this room is
              somebody who can't stop talking about
              problems.

The temperature in the room drops. Anastasia's eyes bore into
Genovese. The table goes silent.

                         GENOVESE
              I'm raising a legitimate concern.
              Every man here has the right to hear
              it before he votes.

                         ANASTASIA
              Every man here already knows what you
              are.

                         GENOVESE
                    (controlled)
              And what's that?

                         ANASTASIA
              You're the guy who ran to Italy when
              things got hot. You sat out the whole
              war over there while the rest of us
              kept things running. And now you come
              back and tell us the boss can't lead
              from a distance?

Genovese's jaw tightens. The room holds its breath.

                         GENOVESE
              That's not what I --

                         ANASTASIA
              You weren't here, Vito. You were
              gone. Charlie was in a cell and he
              ran things better from there than you
              ever ran them from anywhere. So maybe
              sit down and --

                         LUCKY
              Albert.

The single word cuts through. Anastasia stops. Looks at Lucky.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Let the man finish.

A beat. Anastasia sits back. But his eyes stay locked on Genovese.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Vito. You've made your point. The
              families have a right to know their
              boss can lead from here. I understand
              the concern. Now let me answer it.

He takes a step toward Genovese's end of the table.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I've been in Cuba since October. Four
              months. In that time I've built an
              airline, invested in the Nacional and
              the racetrack, set up the entire
              supply line from France, and organized
              this conference -- every man in this
              room, brought together, seated at one
              table. From Cuba.

He lets that land.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              If I can do all that in four months
              from here, I think I can handle a
              union dispute.

Laughter from several bosses. The tension cracks, just slightly.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              The motion is on the table. Boss of
              All Bosses. I'm asking for a vote.
              Anybody wants to second?

Silence. The question hangs.

Then ANASTASIA pushes back his chair. The scrape of wood on tile
sounds like a gunshot in the quiet room. He rises to his full height
-- six feet, two hundred and thirty pounds, the man who runs Murder
Inc. Every eye in the room follows him up.

He looks at Lucky. Then at Genovese. Then he slowly turns his gaze
across every face at the table, one by one. His right hand drops to
his side, resting near his waist.

The room doesn't breathe.

                         ANASTASIA
              For me, you are the Big Boss,
              whether you like it or not. That's
              the way I look at it, and I would
              like to hear from anybody who don't
              feel the same way.

Dead silence.

His eyes continue moving from face to face. Each man he looks at
holds his gaze for a moment, then drops it. Nobody speaks. Nobody
moves.

                         ANASTASIA (CONT'D)
              I second the motion.

He sits. The chair groans under his weight.

The silence holds for three long seconds. Then Lucky nods.

                         LUCKY
              Thank you, Albert.
                    (to the room)
              All in favor.

Hands go up. Costello first. Then Lansky. Bonanno. Trafficante.
Moretti. Adonis. The Fischettis. One by one, every hand in the room
rises.

Genovese raises his hand last. His face is a careful mask.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Unanimous.

Lucky walks slowly down the length of the table. His footsteps are
the only sound. He stops in front of Genovese. Extends his hand.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Vito. We're all together on this.
              Right?

Genovese looks at Lucky's outstretched hand. A beat that lasts a
lifetime. The room watches.

Genovese stands. Takes Lucky's hand. They shake. Lucky holds the grip
a moment longer than necessary -- long enough for every man at the
table to see who is holding and who is being held.

                         GENOVESE
              Together.

Lucky releases him. Pats Genovese once on the shoulder -- a gesture
that looks friendly and is anything but. Genovese sits. His jaw is
set like concrete.

Lucky walks back to the head of the table. The question is answered.
The authority confirmed. His bearing shifts -- from politician to
executive. Now he is purely the Boss.

                         LUCKY
              Good. Now let's talk about money.
              Meyer.

Lansky stands. Opens his leather notebook.

                         LANSKY
              Gentlemen. The China route is
              collapsing. Communist forces control
              most of the opium-producing regions.
              Within three years, that supply is
              gone. We need a replacement, and
              we've built one.

He speaks with the precision of an accountant delivering quarterly
results.

                         LANSKY (CONT'D)
              The new pipeline. Opium from Turkey
              and Lebanon, moved to processing
              facilities in Sicily. Morphine base
              from Sicily goes to laboratories in
              Southern France -- Marseille,
              primarily -- where Corsican operators
              process it into pure heroin. From
              Marseille, shipments come to Havana.
              From Havana, distribution to U.S.
              ports: Tampa, New Orleans, New York.

He pauses. Lets the architecture settle.

                         LANSKY (CONT'D)
              Projected annual revenue, fully
              operational: fifty million dollars.

The number lands like a hammer. Bosses exchange glances. Fifty
million.

                         TRAFFICANTE
              When does this start moving?

                         LANSKY
              Corsican contacts are in place.
              Sicily processing is operational.
              First major shipments through Cuba by
              spring. Full capacity by year's end.

                         LUCKY
              Territories. Every family gets a
              distribution zone. Tampa goes to
              Trafficante. New Orleans -- Marcello
              and Costello share it. New York gets
              divided among the five families,
              proportional to what you invest.
              Nobody steps on anybody else's
              territory. Disputes come to me.

                         BONANNO
              And the Cuba hub? Who controls it?

                         LUCKY
              Meyer and I run Cuba. Not up for
              discussion.

Bonanno nods. No objections.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Each family puts in its share.
              Returns are proportional. Meyer
              handles the books. Questions go to
              him. Problems come to me. That's how
              this works.

He looks around the room one final time.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Gentlemen. We just built something
              that'll outlast all of us.

He raises his glass.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Salute.

                         ALL
              Salute.

They drink. The session breaks. Bosses stand, cluster into groups,
shake hands, light fresh cigars. The murmur of twenty men dividing a
continental empire fills the room.

CLOSE ON GENOVESE -- alone near the far wall. He straightens his
tie. His face is composed. Almost pleasant. But his eyes track Lucky
across the room with the fixed patience of a man who has just decided
exactly where to aim.

He buttons his jacket. Walks out. The door closes behind him.

Lucky, across the room, watches him leave. His smile holds for the
benefit of the men around him. But for a single unguarded moment, his
eyes are not smiling at all.


----------------------------------------
# Scene 13 - Christmas Eve
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - CONFERENCE ROOM - NIGHT (DECEMBER 24, 1946)

Christmas Eve. A smaller session -- eight bosses around the conference
table instead of twenty. Christmas garlands hang incongruously from
the chandeliers. Through the windows, the lights of Havana twinkle.

LUCKY LUCIANO sits at the head. MEYER LANSKY beside him. ANASTASIA,
COSTELLO, TRAFFICANTE, MORETTI, ADONIS, and BONANNO fill the other
chairs. Genovese is absent.

Moretti slaps a newspaper on the table. A headline about Las Vegas
construction.

                         MORETTI
              Six million dollars. The man's spent
              six million dollars on a hotel in the
              desert. Our six million dollars.

                         ADONIS
              The budget was one million. One.
              Where'd the other five go?

                         TRAFFICANTE
              Into his girlfriend's closet, is
              where. Virginia Hill's been spending
              money like she prints it.

                         MORETTI
              He's skimming. Everybody knows he's
              skimming. The question is what are we
              gonna do about it.

                         ANASTASIA
              I say we do what needs doing. Send
              somebody to Vegas and finish it.

                         MORETTI
              I second that.

                         BONANNO
              The man's out of control. Six million
              on a building that isn't even
              finished. We're investors, not
              suckers.

Lucky listens. Lets them talk. His eyes move to Lansky.

Lansky's face is composed. Perfectly still. He studies the table in
front of him.

                         LUCKY
              Meyer. You got the numbers.

                         LANSKY
              The Flamingo opens in two days.
              December twenty-sixth. Siegel's got
              the talent booked, the tables ready,
              the press invited. If the opening
              goes well -- if the money comes in --
              we get our investment back and then
              some.

                         MORETTI
              And if it doesn't?

                         LANSKY
              If it doesn't, we deal with it then.
              But killing Ben now means writing off
              six million dollars. That money's
              gone forever. Give him the opening.
              Two days. What's two days?

                         ANASTASIA
              Meyer, the man's been stealing from
              us.

                         LANSKY
              Maybe. Maybe he's a bad accountant.
              Maybe his contractor's robbing him
              blind and he doesn't know it. It
              doesn't matter. What matters is the
              Flamingo. If it works, the money
              comes back. If it doesn't --

He looks at Anastasia.

                         LANSKY (CONT'D)
              Then I won't stand in your way.

A beat. The room weighs it.

                         LUCKY
              Two days. We wait for the opening. If
              the Flamingo's a hit, Siegel pays
              back every dime and we move forward.
              If it's not...

He doesn't finish the sentence. He doesn't need to.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              But nobody moves on Ben until we know.
              That's my decision. Anybody disagree?

Looks around the table. Moretti shakes his head reluctantly. Anastasia
shrugs. Bonanno nods.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Good. Merry Christmas, gentlemen.

The bosses push back from the table. The session is over.

Lansky stays seated. He folds the newspaper carefully, creasing it
along the headline about Las Vegas. His hands are steady. His face
gives nothing away.

Lucky places a hand briefly on Lansky's shoulder as he passes. Lansky
doesn't look up.

EXT. HOTEL NACIONAL - GARDENS - NIGHT

Christmas lights strung through palm trees. The distant sound of
music from the hotel bar -- "Silent Night" played by a Cuban trio.

CARLOS DELGADO stands near a stone balustrade overlooking the
Malacon, smoking a cigarette. His jacket is off, his tie loosened.
He's been on duty for three days straight and it shows.

JACK BRENNAN approaches from the garden path, hands in his pockets.
He stops a few feet away, as if admiring the view.

                         BRENNAN
              Nice night.

Delgado glances at him. Recognizes him from the bar.

                         DELGADO
              You're the textile man. Burke.

                         BRENNAN
              That's right.

                         DELGADO
              You don't look like a textile man.

                         BRENNAN
              What do I look like?

                         DELGADO
              Like a man who watches people for a
              living.

A beat. Brennan takes a chance.

                         BRENNAN
              Takes one to know one.

Delgado's expression doesn't change. He takes a drag on his
cigarette.

                         DELGADO
              What do you want?

                         BRENNAN
              I think we're watching the same
              things. I think you've been inside
              that conference room and you've heard
              what I can only guess at. And I think
              it bothers you.

                         DELGADO
              You don't know anything about me.

                         BRENNAN
              I know you're Secret Police. I know
              you've been assigned to Luciano's
              detail. And I know you're standing
              out here alone on Christmas Eve
              instead of home with your family.
              That tells me something.

Delgado looks at him sharply.

                         DELGADO
              Who are you?

                         BRENNAN
              Federal Bureau of Narcotics. I'm here
              to document what's happening in that
              hotel. And I need someone on the
              inside.

                         DELGADO
              You're asking me to betray my
              assignment.

                         BRENNAN
              I'm asking you to tell me what's
              going on in those rooms. What they're
              planning. What they're building. I've
              got pieces. You've got the picture.

                         DELGADO
              And if I say no?

                         BRENNAN
              Then you say no and I walk away and
              we never had this conversation. But
              you won't say no.

                         DELGADO
              You're very sure of yourself.

                         BRENNAN
              I'm sure of you. You've seen what
              these men are. You've been in the
              room. The drugs, the money, the
              territories they're carving up --
              you've heard it all. And you're out
              here, smoking alone, on Christmas
              Eve. That's a man with a conscience
              he can't quite silence.

Delgado is quiet for a long moment. He looks out at the dark water.
The music drifts from the bar. "Silent Night."

                         DELGADO
              I have a wife. Two daughters. If
              anyone finds out I'm talking to
              an American agent --

                         BRENNAN
              No one finds out. You talk to me.
              Only me. No records with your name.
              No one in Washington knows who you
              are. I protect my sources. That's
              not a promise -- that's how I work.

Another long beat. Delgado drops his cigarette. Grinds it under his
shoe.

                         DELGADO
              What do you want to know?

                         BRENNAN
              Everything.

                         DELGADO
              There's a man in that hotel.
              Luciano's underboss. Genovese. You
              should be watching him, not Luciano.

                         BRENNAN
              Luciano's the target.

                         DELGADO
              Luciano has rules. Genovese has
              none. I've been around both of them
              for months. Trust me on this.

Brennan studies him.

                         BRENNAN
              I'll keep that in mind. But right
              now -- the conference. What was
              decided?

Delgado looks back at the hotel. The lights. The music. The men
inside who hold the fate of nations in their hands and don't care.

                         DELGADO
              Sit down. This is going to take a
              while.

They sit on the stone balustrade, two dark figures against the
Christmas lights, and Delgado begins to talk.

The camera pulls back slowly -- the garden, the hotel glowing behind
them, the ocean beyond. Two men on a bench, deciding the future,
while "Silent Night" plays on.

# Scene 14 - Christmas Day
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - CORRIDOR - EARLY MORNING (DECEMBER 25, 1946)

Christmas morning. The hotel is quiet. Garlands and holly drape the
hallway sconces. A room service cart sits abandoned outside a door.

VITO GENOVESE walks down the corridor in his shirtsleeves, checking
over his shoulder. He finds an empty service alcove near the back
stairwell. A telephone on the wall.

He picks up the receiver. Dials. Waits. Checks the corridor again.

                         GENOVESE
                    (low, controlled)
              It's me. I don't have long.

He cups his hand around the mouthpiece.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              The conference is winding down. I've
              got everything. Attendees, the
              structure, the supply routes.
              Luciano's running the whole operation
              from here -- heroin, gambling, all of
              it. He's got the Corsicans building a
              pipeline from France.

He listens.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              Every boss in America was in that
              room. I can give you names, dates,
              territories. Everything your director
              needs to make a case.

He listens again. A thin smile crosses his face.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              You tell Hartley that Luciano is
              running a narcotics empire from a
              mansion in Havana. That's all he
              needs to hear. The rest is details.

He glances down the corridor. A MAID pushes a cart around the far
corner. Genovese turns away from her, lowers his voice further.

                         GENOVESE (CONT'D)
              I'll call again when I'm back in New
              York. Merry Christmas.

He hangs up. Straightens his collar. Walks back down the corridor as
if he's just been out for a morning stroll.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - LOBBY - DAY (DECEMBER 26, 1946)

The day after Christmas. Suitcases in the lobby. PORTERS loading
luggage onto carts. Some bosses are already departing -- handshakes,
embraces, promises to stay in touch.

Lucky stands near the front desk with Lansky, seeing off guests.
Costello approaches, adjusts his coat.

                         COSTELLO
              Hell of a week, Charlie.

                         LUCKY
              Come back anytime, Frank. The weather's
              better than New York.

                         COSTELLO
                    (smiling)
              The company's about the same.

They shake hands. Costello walks out. Behind him, Anastasia claps
Lucky on the shoulder.

                         ANASTASIA
              You need me, you call. Day or night.

                         LUCKY
              I know, Albert. Safe trip home.

Anastasia nods and follows the porters out.

TRAFFICANTE approaches Lansky, shakes his hand.

                         TRAFFICANTE
              Meyer, when the pipeline's ready,
              Tampa's open. Whatever you need moved
              through my port.

                         LANSKY
              I'll be in touch.

The lobby empties. The conference is over. The last cars pull away
from the hotel entrance.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - LANSKY'S SUITE - EVENING

Lansky sits in an armchair, the evening paper spread before him. Lucky
paces by the window, a drink in his hand. The suite feels empty after
days of crowded rooms.

A KNOCK. Lucky opens the door. MORETTI stands there, agitated,
holding a telegram.

                         MORETTI
              You're gonna want to see this.

He hands the telegram to Lucky. Lucky reads it. His jaw tightens.

                         LUCKY
              Meyer.

He hands it to Lansky. Lansky reads. His face goes very still.

                         LANSKY
              Opening night. Disaster.

                         MORETTI
              The celebrities didn't show.
              Gamblers cleaned out the house and
              the reserves couldn't cover it. Half
              the hotel isn't even finished --
              people could hear construction from
              their rooms. It's a catastrophe.

                         LUCKY
              How bad?

                         MORETTI
              They're talking about shutting it
              down within two weeks. The losses
              from one night --

He shakes his head.

                         MORETTI (CONT'D)
              It's bad, Charlie. Real bad.

Lucky turns to Lansky. Lansky is still staring at the telegram. His
expression hasn't changed, but his hand, holding the paper, has gone
white at the knuckles.

                         LUCKY
              Meyer.

                         LANSKY
                    (quietly)
              I heard.

                         MORETTI
              The boys are gonna want blood. You
              know that. Six million of their money
              in a building that can't even keep
              its lights on.

                         LUCKY
              Nobody moves on Ben.

                         MORETTI
              Charlie --

                         LUCKY
              Nobody. Moves. On Ben. Not yet.

He takes the telegram from Lansky. Folds it. Puts it in his pocket.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              We gave him until the opening. The
              opening was a disaster. Fine. But the
              Flamingo's still standing. The tables
              are still there. The rooms are still
              there. You kill the man, you kill the
              project. Six million, gone. No chance
              of getting any of it back.

                         MORETTI
              And if it doesn't turn around?

                         LUCKY
              Then it doesn't turn around. And
              we'll handle it. But not now. Not
              over one bad night.

He looks at Moretti.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              You pass that along. To everybody.
              Siegel gets until spring. If the
              Flamingo can't make money by then,
              I'll sign the order myself.

Moretti looks at Lansky. Lansky nods almost imperceptibly.

                         MORETTI
              All right. Spring.

He leaves. The door closes.

Lucky and Lansky are alone. A long silence.

                         LANSKY
              It won't turn around.

                         LUCKY
              Maybe not.

                         LANSKY
              Ben was always -- he could sell
              anything. The big ideas, the vision.
              But the details... the details
              always killed him.

                         LUCKY
              I bought you time, Meyer. That's all
              I can do.

Lansky folds his hands. Stares at nothing.

                         LANSKY
              I know.

Lucky finishes his drink. Sets the glass down.

                         LUCKY
              This conference. The vote.
              Genovese. The pipeline. Siegel. And
              now this.

He walks to the window. Looks out at the Havana night. Lights on the
water. Music from somewhere far away.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Five days. We built an empire and
              watched it crack in the same week.

                         LANSKY
              It hasn't cracked. The pipeline's
              solid. The territories are set. The
              vote held. Siegel's a side issue.

                         LUCKY
              Siegel's not the crack I'm worried
              about.

Lansky looks at him. Understands.

                         LANSKY
              Genovese.

                         LUCKY
              He voted with us. He shook my hand.
              And I don't trust him one inch further
              than I can see him.

                         LANSKY
              Neither do I.

Lucky turns from the window.

                         LUCKY
              Keep your eyes open, Meyer. Watch who
              he talks to. Watch where his money
              goes. A man that hungry doesn't stop
              eating just because you told him to.

                         LANSKY
              I will.

Lucky nods. Walks to the door. Stops.

                         LUCKY
              Merry Christmas, Meyer.

                         LANSKY
              Merry Christmas, Charlie.

Lucky leaves. Lansky sits alone in the suite. He picks up the
telegram. Reads it one more time. Then he folds it very carefully
and puts it in his breast pocket, close to his chest.

He reaches for the Scotch. Pours himself a glass. Drinks it slowly,
looking at nothing.

# Scene 15 - The Assassination Attempt
----------------------------------------

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - NIGHT (LATE DECEMBER 1946)

The mansion glows softly against the dark. Palm trees sway in the
ocean breeze. Waves break somewhere beyond the garden wall. The
neighborhood is quiet -- wealthy, residential, peaceful.

A black car idles on the street, lights off. Two more are parked half
a block away. The engines are running.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - LIVING ROOM - CONTINUOUS

LUCKY LUCIANO sits in an armchair reading a newspaper, a glass of
wine on the side table. The room is elegant -- high ceilings, tile
floors, heavy wooden furniture. A radio plays low Cuban music.

CARLOS DELGADO stands by the front window, parting the curtain
slightly, scanning the street. Routine check. He lets the curtain
fall.

                         DELGADO
              Quiet night.

                         LUCKY
              They're all quiet now. The circus
              left town.

                         DELGADO
              You don't miss the company?

                         LUCKY
              I miss the business. The company I
              can do without.

Lucky turns a page. Delgado moves to the kitchen doorway, exchanges
a word with a HOUSEMAN. Returns.

                         DELGADO
              I'm going to check the back gate.
              Five minutes.

                         LUCKY
              Take your time.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - GARDEN - CONTINUOUS

Delgado walks through the garden toward the rear wall. Lush tropical
plants, a stone path, the sound of the ocean growing louder. He
reaches the back gate. Checks the lock. Scans the dark alley beyond.

Nothing. He turns back toward the house.

Then he stops. Cocks his head. Through the sound of waves and wind,
something else -- the scrape of shoes on pavement. Quiet. Deliberate.
Coming from the front.

Delgado draws his sidearm. Moves quickly along the side of the house,
staying in the shadows.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - FRONT - CONTINUOUS

THREE GUNMEN in dark clothes approach the front entrance. They move
with professional coordination -- one at the door, two flanking. The
lead man carries a Thompson submachine gun. The others have pistols.

The lead gunman raises the Thompson.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - LIVING ROOM - CONTINUOUS

Lucky hears something. He looks up from his paper. The radio plays.
The night is still.

Then the front windows EXPLODE.

Automatic gunfire tears through the living room. The window glass
shatters inward. Bullets chew across the walls, the furniture, the
plaster. The radio disintegrates. Lucky's wine glass bursts on the
side table.

Lucky throws himself from the chair, hitting the tile floor and
rolling behind a heavy wooden cabinet. Bullets punch through the
wall above him, showering him with plaster dust.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - SIDE YARD - CONTINUOUS

Delgado sprints along the wall, weapon up. He comes around the corner
and sees the gunmen -- three figures, muzzle flashes lighting up the
front of the house.

He fires twice. The flanking gunman on the right staggers, clutches
his shoulder, stumbles into the hedges.

The lead gunman spins toward Delgado, raking the Thompson across the
front garden. Bullets chop through palm fronds and stone.

Delgado dives behind a low garden wall. Fires back. The second
flanking gunman drops behind a car.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - LIVING ROOM - CONTINUOUS

Lucky crawls across the floor, keeping below the window line. Glass
crunches under his hands and knees. He reaches a heavy desk against
the far wall, pulls open the drawer. Takes out a pistol.

The gunfire outside shifts -- it's no longer directed at the house.
Delgado is engaging them.

EXT. MIRAMAR MANSION - FRONT - CONTINUOUS

The lead gunman empties the Thompson's drum toward Delgado's position.
The stone wall chips and cracks. Then the gun clicks empty.

Delgado rises. Fires three times. The lead gunman is hit in the leg
and goes down hard on the pavement.

The remaining gunman grabs his wounded partner and drags him toward
the waiting car. The third man -- the one Delgado hit first -- is
already being hauled into the second vehicle.

Tires screech. The cars accelerate away into the dark, headlights
still off.

Silence. Then the sound of the ocean again. Dogs barking somewhere in
the neighborhood. A light goes on in a house down the street.

Delgado stands. Breathing hard. His hands are steady but his face is
ashen. He walks to the front door.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - LIVING ROOM - CONTINUOUS

The room is destroyed. Bullet holes across every wall. The windows
are gone. Broken glass covers the floor like ice. Plaster dust floats
in the air. The newspaper Lucky was reading has been shredded by
gunfire.

Lucky stands behind the desk, pistol in his hand, white plaster dust
in his hair and across his shoulders. He looks at the devastation
with controlled fury -- his jaw set, his eyes burning.

Delgado appears in the doorway, weapon still drawn.

                         DELGADO
              Are you hit?

                         LUCKY
              No.

                         DELGADO
              Three men. Professional. They had a
              Thompson and two handguns. I hit two
              of them. They had cars waiting.

Lucky sets the pistol on the desk. Brushes plaster dust from his
sleeves with deliberate calm.

                         LUCKY
              Professional.

                         DELGADO
              Not local. The car had New York
              plates.

Lucky looks up. His eyes are cold and very still.

                         LUCKY
              New York plates.

He walks through the wreckage of his living room. Steps on broken

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I know who sent them.

                         DELGADO
              Who?

                         LUCKY
              It doesn't matter. Not yet. Can't
              prove it. Won't be able to prove it.
              That's how he works.

He turns from the window. Faces Delgado.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              You saved my life tonight.

                         DELGADO
              I did my job.

                         LUCKY
              Your job was standing by a window.
              Going out there -- that was something
              else.

A beat. Two men looking at each other across a bullet-riddled room.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              From now on, you're with me. Not the
              house. Not the hotel. Me. Wherever I
              go, you go. Understand?

                         DELGADO
              I understand.

                         LUCKY
              Good.

He picks a shard of glass from his sleeve. Lets it drop to the floor.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Get somebody to board up these
              windows. And call Lansky. Tell him
              what happened. Tell him --

He pauses. Looks at the destroyed room one more time.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              Tell him Cuba isn't as safe as we
              thought.

Delgado nods. Holsters his weapon. Walks to the telephone in the
hallway -- miraculously intact amid the destruction.

Lucky stands alone in the ruined living room. His paradise, punctured
by bullets. His face reveals nothing. But his hand, hanging at his
side, is clenched into a fist so tight the knuckles are white.

Outside, the ocean continues its indifferent rhythm. The palm trees
sway. Havana sleeps. And somewhere on a dark road heading west, three
wounded men drive toward the airport with a failed contract and a
story they will never tell.

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - KITCHEN - LATER

The windows are boarded. Glass has been swept into piles. The houseman
works quietly. Lucky sits at the kitchen table -- the only undamaged
room -- a glass of wine untouched in front of him.

Delgado enters. Lucky looks up. Studies him.

                         LUCKY
              Sit down, Carlos.

Delgado sits. Lucky pours him a glass. Pushes it across the table.
A gesture he has never made before -- the boss serving the bodyguard.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              I know who sent those men. I can't
              prove it. Under the rules, suspicion
              isn't enough. You need evidence.
              You need witnesses. You need a vote.

He turns the glass in his hands.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              That's the system I built. Those are
              the rules I asked every boss in that
              room to follow. And now those rules
              protect the man who's trying to kill me.

                         DELGADO
              Then change the rules.

                         LUCKY
              If I change the rules for myself, they
              mean nothing. Then I'm just another
              Genovese.

A long silence. Lucky drinks. Delgado drinks. Two men in a boarded-up
kitchen, one of them realizing that the system he created is also his
cage.

                         LUCKY (CONT'D)
              From now on, Carlos -- you and me.
              Nobody else comes close. Not staff,
              not associates, nobody I haven't
              known for twenty years. Understand?

                         DELGADO
              I understand.

Lucky nods. The circle of trust has shrunk to a man who is secretly
reporting everything to the FBN.

dates, room numbers.

Brennan and DELGADO sit at an outdoor table overlooking the harbor.
Coffee cups. Brennan has a small notebook open. Delgado speaks in a

Delgado slides a folded scrap of paper across the table. Brennan

                         DELGADO
              The supply routes -- I heard them

              I think Genovese lost a vote and got
              his hand shaken in front of twenty
              bosses. And I think a man like that

Brennan considers this.

                         BRENNAN
              Genovese is secondary. Luciano's the
              target.

                         DELGADO
              That's your assignment. I understand.
              But I'm telling you something you
              don't want to hear.

He looks Brennan in the eye.

                         DELGADO (CONT'D)
              I've been with Luciano for three

              code. He doesn't move on people
              without reason, and he doesn't let
              his men act like animals. Genovese --

He shakes his head.

                         DELGADO (CONT'D)
              Genovese has no rules. None. I've
              seen the way he looks at people. Like
              they're furniture. Like they're

              My brother died with a needle in his

              I'm not asking you to. I'm telling

A beat. The words land. Brennan stares at his notebook.

                         BRENNAN
              That's not my call to make.

                         DELGADO
              Whose call is it?

                         BRENNAN
              Washington's. I gather the evidence.

Brennan doesn't answer. He closes his notebook.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - NIGHT

Brennan on the phone again. This call is different -- his voice is

                         BRENNAN
              Director, I need to flag something

Blazing Caribbean sun. The grandstand is packed with SPECTATORS in

LUCKY LUCIANO stands at the rail of the Jockey Club section, elevated

Lucky and Sinatra at a table on a restaurant patio. Drinks, cigars,

A KNOCK. An AIDE enters.

              Luciano. It's going to be in every

              The full file. It's time.

The aide leaves. Hartley looks at the newspaper photographs one more

INT. STATE DEPARTMENT - CONFERENCE ROOM - DAY

A wood-paneled room. American flags. HARTLEY sits across a polished

in front of him.

              Gentlemen. You've seen the

              The phones haven't stopped ringing.

              Cuba.

              Because he is living like a king in
              Cuba. And it's worse than the photos

He opens the folder. Spreads documents across the table.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)

              complete narcotics infrastructure.
              Luciano hosted the largest organized

              They've built a heroin pipeline from
              Turkey through France to Cuba --
              projected revenue, fifty million
              dollars a year. Cuba is the hub. And
              Luciano is running it all from a

              An ultimatum.

The room is quiet.

              The alternative is explaining to the

Hartley nods. Closes his folder. Stands.

                         HARTLEY
              Gentlemen. By March, Lucky Luciano

              guarantee it.

He walks out. The door closes behind him. The officials sit in

the Cuban sun.

EXT. ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - GRANDSTAND - DAY

Back in Havana. Another day at the races. Lucky and Sinatra at the

his glass.

And the cameras keep flashing.

INT. CUBAN PRESIDENTIAL PALACE - EARLIER THAT DAY

FLASHBACK - THAT MORNING

An ornate office. PRESIDENT BATISTA, 50s, sits behind a massive desk,

              Cuba. He has broken no Cuban laws.

Batista stares at the document. His hands grip the edge of the desk.

                         BATISTA

                         AMBASSADOR
              A straightforward arrangement.

                         BATISTA

              revenue --

                         AMBASSADOR
              We're aware of Mr. Luciano's

              The ultimatum. State Department
              delivered it this morning. Cuba
              signed off four hours later. Batista
              had no choice. Luciano is going to
              be deported.

Brennan sits on the edge of his bed. The words sink in.

                         BRENNAN
              When?

                         HARTLEY
              Soon. Weeks, not months. They'll
              arrest him, detain him at Tiscornia,
              and put him on a boat back to Italy.
              Your evidence made this possible,
              Jack. The conference intelligence,
              the heroin routes, the financial
              records -- it was the foundation of
              the entire case.

                         BRENNAN
              What about the network? The pipeline
              from France? Deporting Luciano
              doesn't shut that down.

                         HARTLEY
              One thing at a time. Get the head of
              the snake and the body dies. That's
              how this works.

                         BRENNAN
              Director, my contact says --

                         HARTLEY
              Your contact is a Cuban policeman,
              Jack. He doesn't see the big picture.
              We're taking down the most powerful
              organized crime figure in the Western
              Hemisphere. This is the biggest action
              the FBN has ever taken. Your name is
              on it. Be proud of that.

Brennan is quiet for a moment.

                         BRENNAN
              I am proud.

                         HARTLEY
              You should be. Your brother -- this
              is what he would have wanted.

The mention of his brother hits like it always does. Brennan closes
his eyes briefly.

                         BRENNAN
              Yes, sir.

                         HARTLEY
              Stay in position. When the arrest
              happens, I want you there.
              Documenting. This is history, Jack.
              You're part of it.

                         BRENNAN
              Understood.

                         HARTLEY
              Good man.

The line goes dead. Brennan holds the receiver. Sets it down slowly.

He looks at his corkboard. The web of names and photographs and red
string. The case he built. The work of months.

He should feel triumph. He feels something else -- something he can't
name. He reaches for the photograph of Genovese, still facedown on
the desk where he left it weeks ago. Turns it over. Studies the face.

Then he puts it back facedown. Picks up his notebook. Starts writing.

EXT. HAVANA - MALECON SEAWALL - DUSK

Brennan walks along the Malecon. The sea is rough, waves crashing
over the wall, spray misting the air. The sun is setting, painting
the sky red and orange.

DELGADO falls into step beside him.

                         DELGADO
              I heard. The ultimatum.

                         BRENNAN
              News travels fast.

                         DELGADO
              In Cuba, everything travels fast.
              Except justice.

They walk.

                         BRENNAN
              It's done, Delgado. Luciano's going
              to be deported. The case I built --
              your information, my informants,
              all of it -- it worked.

                         DELGADO
              It worked.

                         BRENNAN
              You don't sound convinced.

                         DELGADO
              I'm convinced the Americans got what
              they wanted. I'm not convinced it's
              what Cuba needed. Or what America
              needs, for that matter.

                         BRENNAN
              We're taking down the man who built
              the French Connection. The man who's
              going to flood American cities with
              heroin.

                         DELGADO
              You're taking down the man who built
              it with rules. The man who replaces
              him won't have any.

They stop at the seawall. Waves crash below. The sky bleeds red.

                         BRENNAN
              What would you have me do? Walk away?
              My brother is dead because of
              Luciano's heroin. I came here to stop
              that. And I stopped it.

                         DELGADO
              Did you?

The question hangs between them. Brennan has no answer.

                         DELGADO (CONT'D)
              When Luciano is gone, who runs the
              pipeline? Who controls the heroin?
              It won't be nobody, Brennan. It'll
              be somebody. And the somebody who's
              been positioning himself for this
              moment since December is Vito
              Genovese.

                         BRENNAN
              Genovese is not my --

He stops himself. He's heard those words before. From Hartley.
"Genovese is not your assignment." The echo hits him. He goes quiet.

                         DELGADO
              You were about to say he's not your
              assignment.

Brennan stares at the water.

                         BRENNAN
              Yeah. I was.

A long beat. The waves. The sunset. The city behind them, going dark.

                         DELGADO
              You're a good man, Brennan. You did
              good work. But sometimes good men
              do the wrong thing for the right
              reasons. And by the time they figure
              it out, it's too late to fix.

Brennan says nothing. He looks out at the darkening ocean -- the
same ocean that will carry Lucky Luciano away from Cuba forever.

The sun drops below the horizon. The sky turns from red to purple
to black. The lights of Havana reflect on the water.

Somewhere in Washington, Hartley is celebrating. Somewhere in New
York, Genovese is waiting. And here, on the Malecon, two men who
helped bring down an empire stand in the dark and wonder what they've
built in its place.

                                                       END OF ACT TWO.

# Scene 19 - The Ultimatum Delivered
----------------------------------------

INT. MIRAMAR MANSION - SITTING ROOM - DAY (FEBRUARY 21, 1947)

SUPER: FEBRUARY 21, 1947

The same Miramar mansion. The same bougainvillea climbing the columns. The same Caribbean
light flooding through the windows. But the house feels different now -- quieter, emptier, as
though it already knows what's coming.

LUCKY LUCIANO sits in an armchair by the window, reading a newspaper. The Havana morning is
beautiful. Coffee on the side table, half-finished. A man in his routine.

A car pulls up outside. Lucky glances through the window. He recognizes the car.

The front door opens. Footsteps on the marble floor -- measured, heavy. Not the brisk, eager
stride Lucky remembers from five months ago.

MINISTER ALFREDO VALDES appears in the doorway. He wears the same impeccable guayabera. The
same gold watch. But the smile is gone. In its place, something Lucky has never seen on the
minister's face: dread.

Lucky sets down the newspaper. He reads Valdes in an instant.

                    LUCKY
          Alfredo.

                    VALDES
          Senor Luciano.

A beat. Valdes doesn't move from the doorway. Doesn't come to shake hands. Doesn't cross the
room with his arm extended the way he did that first day at the airport. He just stands there.

                    LUCKY
          Sit down.

Valdes crosses to the chair opposite Lucky. The same chairs where they first discussed
arrangements, residency papers, the mansion. He sits, but doesn't settle. His hands rest on
his knees. He doesn't reach for the coffee.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          You look like a man who drove here wishing
          the road was longer.

                    VALDES
          I did.

A long silence. Lucky waits. He's always been good at reading rooms.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          I received a communication from the
          president's office this morning. It concerns
          you directly.
               (beat)
          The United States government has delivered an
          ultimatum to Cuba.

Lucky doesn't react. Not yet. His eyes stay on Valdes.

                    VALDES (CONT'D)
          All shipments of medical supplies to Cuba
          will be suspended immediately unless you are
          deported. All of them. Morphine, anesthesia,
          penicillin, antibiotics. Everything our
          hospitals depend on. Everything that keeps
          our people alive.

Lucky processes this. The scale of it. Not a diplomatic request. Not a negotiation. A gun to
Cuba's head.

                    LUCKY
          They're holding your hospitals hostage.

                    VALDES
          Yes.

                    LUCKY
          How long before you run out?

                    VALDES
          Weeks. Some things, days. There is no
          secondary supplier. Cuba buys everything
          from the Americans. They know this.

Lucky stands. Walks to the window. Looks out at the ocean -- the same view he admired the first
day Lansky brought him here. Florida is out there, ninety miles away. The country that exiled
him, that followed him here, that is now using an island nation's healthcare as leverage to
finish what it started.

                    LUCKY
          Batista sent you.

                    VALDES
          The president has no choice. Neither do I.

                    LUCKY
          I'm not angry at you, Alfredo.

Valdes blinks. He expected rage. Accusations. The reaction of a powerful man being told his
power is over. Instead, Lucky turns from the window with something almost like understanding.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Five months ago you walked into an airport
          lounge and handed me residency papers. You
          arranged this house. You smoothed every
          permit, every introduction. You opened every
          door on this island.

                    VALDES
          I did.

                    LUCKY
          And now the Americans tell you to close them.

                    VALDES
          The Americans have left us no choice.

Lucky nods slowly. He walks back to his chair and sits.

                    LUCKY
          You know what this is, don't you? This isn't
          about me. It's not about drugs or crime or
          whatever they're telling the newspapers.

                    VALDES
          What is it?

                    LUCKY
          Power. They want to show the world that when
          America says jump, even Cuba asks how high.
          I'm just the excuse.

Valdes says nothing. He knows Lucky is right. He's always known how this system works -- he
built his career inside it. But the system just turned on him.

                    VALDES
          I want you to know... this is not what I
          wanted. When I welcomed you to Cuba, it was
          genuine. You were good for this country.
          The investment, the business, the --

                    LUCKY
          The envelopes.

A bitter smile crosses Valdes's face.

                    VALDES
          The envelopes, yes. All of it. It worked. It
          was working. And then the Americans decided
          it should stop.

                    LUCKY
          When?

                    VALDES
          The order is immediate. You will be asked to
          present yourself to authorities within days.
          I am told the arrest will be... public.

                    LUCKY
          Public.

                    VALDES
          The Americans need photographs. Proof that
          Cuba is complying. I am sorry.

Lucky looks at the coffee on the side table. Picks up the cup. Takes a slow sip. Sets it down.

                    LUCKY
          You did what you could, Alfredo. You gave
          me a hundred and forty-one days. That's more
          than anyone else offered.

Valdes stands. He reaches inside his jacket -- the same gesture from their first meeting, when
Lucky's envelope disappeared with practiced ease. This time, his hand comes out empty. There is
nothing left to exchange.

                    VALDES
          Goodbye, Senor Luciano. I wish it had ended
          differently.

Lucky stands. For a moment, the two men face each other in the same room where their
arrangement began. The Caribbean light. The ocean view. The beautiful house that belongs to
someone else.

Lucky extends his hand. Valdes shakes it. The grip is firm, genuine -- the handshake of two men
who understood each other from the beginning and understand each other now at the end.

                    LUCKY
          Take care of yourself, Alfredo. When the
          Americans are done with me, they'll start
          looking for new problems to solve.

Valdes holds the handshake a moment longer than necessary. Then he releases it, turns, and
walks out of the sitting room.

Lucky listens to the footsteps on the marble floor. The front door opens and closes. The car
engine starts. Pulls away.

He stands alone in the mansion. The beautiful, borrowed mansion with its ocean view and its
bougainvillea and its staff and its memories of five months as a king.

He picks up the newspaper. Sets it down again. He doesn't need to read it. He already knows
how the story ends.

The exile king is about to be exiled again.

# Scene 20 - Final Decisions
----------------------------------------

EXT. HAVANA SIDE STREET - NIGHT (FEBRUARY 22, 1947)

SUPER: FEBRUARY 22, 1947

A narrow street in Old Havana. Crumbling colonial walls, a single lamp casting yellow light
over cobblestones. Music drifts from somewhere far away -- a trumpet, a drum, the city that
never stops celebrating even when the ground is shifting beneath it.

JACK BRENNAN leans against a wall in the shadows, smoking. His tie is loosened. He's been
waiting.

Footsteps. Careful ones.

CARLOS DELGADO emerges from the darkness at the end of the street. He walks without hurrying,
checking behind him once, then approaching Brennan with the practiced calm of a man who has
done this before.

They face each other. The last time. Both of them know it.

                    BRENNAN
          Carlos.

                    DELGADO
          Jack.

Brennan drops his cigarette. Grinds it out with his shoe.

                    BRENNAN
          It's happening. Valdes delivered the
          news yesterday. The Cubans are going
          to arrest him.

                    DELGADO
          I know. I was at the mansion this
          morning. He didn't say anything to me,
          but I could see it. He knows it's over.

                    BRENNAN
          We need his schedule. Where he'll be
          tomorrow. Where they can make the arrest
          clean, public, where there are witnesses
          and photographers.

Delgado is quiet for a moment. He looks at the ground. Then up.

                    DELGADO
          Saturday. Tomorrow afternoon. He takes
          Igea to lunch every Saturday at El Jardin.
          Vedado district. He arrives around one
          o'clock. Always the same table, near the
          garden. No guards inside -- I wait by
          the car.

Brennan absorbs this. Every detail filed away.

                    BRENNAN
          How many exits?

                    DELGADO
          Two. Front entrance and a service door
          through the kitchen. He always uses the front.

                    BRENNAN
          Armed?

                    DELGADO
          He never carries a weapon in Havana.
          He thinks he doesn't need one.

Brennan nods. A simple arrest. A man having lunch with his girlfriend in a restaurant. No
shootout, no drama. Just a hand on a shoulder and the words.

                    DELGADO (CONT'D)
          The police will use Hernandez. The
          assistant chief. It is already arranged.
          They want photographs.

                    BRENNAN
          Washington wants photographs.

                    DELGADO
          Everyone wants photographs.

Silence between them. The trumpet has stopped. Just the sound of the city breathing.

                    BRENNAN
          I want you to know -- what you've done,
          Carlos. The risk you've taken. It matters.

                    DELGADO
          Does it?

                    BRENNAN
          We're taking a drug lord off the board.
          The French Connection, the heroin pipeline --
          it starts with him. Remove him and the
          whole structure weakens.

Delgado turns and leans against the wall next to Brennan. Two men side by side in the dark.

                    DELGADO
          I've been with him five months, Jack.
          Every day. Driven him, watched him eat
          breakfast, heard him laugh. Seen him ask
          the gardener about his children. Watched
          him read in the afternoon sun.
               (beat)
          He is a criminal. I know what he is.
          But he is also... a man. And tomorrow
          I help you take away his freedom because
          I gave you his lunch reservation.

                    BRENNAN
          You're doing the right thing.

                    DELGADO
          I told you once that you might be
          creating a bigger monster. Do you
          remember?

                    BRENNAN
          I remember.

                    DELGADO
          Genovese. The man at the conference
          who challenged him. The one who lost
          the vote. That man has no limits, Jack.
          Lucky has a code. Genovese has nothing.
          What happens when the throne is empty?

                    BRENNAN
          That's not our problem. Our problem
          is the man who's been running heroin
          through Cuba for five months.

                    DELGADO
          I hope you're right.

Another silence. Delgado pushes off the wall. Straightens his jacket.

                    DELGADO (CONT'D)
          Tomorrow, after the arrest -- I will be
          there. I will be doing my job. I will be
          the man who was assigned to protect Senor
          Luciano and who stood by while they put
          him in handcuffs. That is what I will be.

                    BRENNAN
          That's the assignment.

                    DELGADO
          The assignment. Yes.
               (beat)
          You and I will not meet again after this.
          You will go back to Washington. I will go
          back to my desk and my family and my
          government salary.

                    BRENNAN
          It's been an honor, Carlos.

Delgado looks at Brennan. Extends his hand. Brennan takes it.

                    DELGADO
          Be careful, Jack. When you get back to
          Washington, ask yourself who wanted this
          more than you did. Ask yourself who
          benefits when Lucky is gone.

Brennan holds the handshake. Something in Delgado's words registers -- not fully, not yet. A
seed of doubt planted in already uncertain soil.

                    BRENNAN
          What do you mean?

                    DELGADO
          I mean that you are a good man who
          believes he is doing the right thing.
          I hope you are. Good night, Jack.

Delgado releases his hand. Turns. Walks back into the darkness the way he came. His footsteps
recede on the cobblestones until there is nothing.

Brennan stands alone in the yellow lamplight. He lights another cigarette. Inhales. Watches the
smoke drift up into the Havana night.

Tomorrow changes everything. The alliance that began on Christmas Eve ends here, on a dark
street, with a handshake and a warning.

He drops the cigarette and walks in the opposite direction.

# Scene 21 - The Arrest
----------------------------------------

EXT. EL JARDIN RESTAURANT - VEDADO DISTRICT - DAY (FEBRUARY 23, 1947)

SUPER: SATURDAY, FEBRUARY 23, 1947

The Vedado district. Tree-lined avenues. A pleasant Saturday afternoon in Havana, the kind
of day that makes exile feel like paradise.

El Jardin -- a white-walled restaurant with an open garden terrace, flowering vines overhead,
wrought-iron tables under the shade of a ceiba tree. The lunch crowd is light. Quiet
conversation, clinking glasses, the distant sound of a radio playing bolero.

A black sedan pulls up. CARLOS DELGADO steps out and opens the rear door.

LUCKY LUCIANO emerges, dressed in a pale gray suit, no hat. IGEA LISSONI follows -- beautiful
in a white dress, hair pinned up, a string of pearls at her throat. She takes Lucky's arm.

They walk toward the entrance. Easy. Unhurried. A couple going to lunch.

Delgado watches them enter. He leans against the car, folds his arms, and scans the street.
His eyes catch something across the avenue -- a MAN with a camera, trying to look casual near a
parked car. Then another, further down, pretending to read a newspaper.

Photographers. Already positioned.

Delgado's jaw tightens. He looks away.

INT. EL JARDIN RESTAURANT - CONTINUOUS

Lucky and Igea are led to their usual table near the garden -- a corner table with a view of
the courtyard, the flowering vines, the afternoon light.

A WAITER approaches. Lucky orders in passable Spanish, gesturing with easy familiarity. He
knows this restaurant, this table, this routine. Every Saturday for months.

                    IGEA
          It's beautiful today.

                    LUCKY
          It's always beautiful here.

                    IGEA
          You seem quiet.

                    LUCKY
          I'm enjoying lunch with a beautiful woman.
          What's quiet about that?

She smiles. Reaches across the table and touches his hand. He covers hers with his own.

The food arrives. Simple, good -- black beans, roasted pork, fried plantains, a bottle of
wine. Lucky eats slowly, savoring it. He looks around the restaurant -- the other diners, the
garden, the light filtering through the vines.

He knows. He may not know it's today, this hour, this table. But since Valdes's visit, he's
known the shape of what's coming. And he has chosen not to run.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          You know what I miss about New York?

                    IGEA
          Everything?

                    LUCKY
          Nothing. Not a thing. New York was
          business. This is living.

He lifts his wine glass. She lifts hers. They touch glasses, a quiet sound in the afternoon
warmth.

EXT. EL JARDIN RESTAURANT - CONTINUOUS

A police car rounds the corner. Unmarked. It pulls to a stop behind Lucky's sedan.

ASSISTANT POLICE CHIEF HERNANDEZ, 50s, uniformed, brass buttons polished, steps out. Two
UNIFORMED OFFICERS follow. Hernandez adjusts his hat and walks toward the restaurant entrance
with the deliberate stride of a man performing for an audience.

Delgado sees him coming. He straightens. Their eyes meet.

Hernandez gives Delgado a small nod -- acknowledgment, nothing more. This is out of Delgado's
hands now.

Delgado looks away. Looks at the ground. The schedule he gave Brennan -- Saturday, one o'clock,
El Jardin, the table near the garden -- delivered to this moment.

The photographers have moved closer. Cameras up.

INT. EL JARDIN RESTAURANT - CONTINUOUS

Lucky is mid-sentence, telling Igea a story. His fork rests on his plate. Wine glass in hand.

The front entrance darkens. Hernandez enters, flanked by his officers. The restaurant goes
quiet. Diners look up. A waiter freezes mid-step.

Hernandez crosses the dining room. His shoes click on the tile floor. Every step measured,
every step photographed through the windows by the men outside.

Lucky sees him. He sets down his wine glass. Slowly. Carefully. The way a man sets down
something he knows he won't pick up again.

                    IGEA
          Charlie --

                    LUCKY
          It's all right.

Hernandez arrives at the table. He removes his hat.

                    HERNANDEZ
          Senor Luciano.

                    LUCKY
          Chief Hernandez.

                    HERNANDEZ
          By order of the President of the Republic
          of Cuba, you are under arrest for violations
          of immigration law. You will be taken to
          the Tiscornia detention facility pending
          deportation proceedings. Please come with
          me.

Lucky looks at Hernandez. Then at the officers behind him. Then at Igea, whose eyes have gone
wide, her hand still reaching across the table toward where his was a moment ago.

Lucky stands. Unhurried. He straightens his jacket. Adjusts his cuffs. The composure of a man
who has been arrested before and knows that dignity is the only thing they can't take from you.

He turns to Igea.

                    LUCKY
          Finish your lunch. The food is good
          today. I'll be fine.

                    IGEA
          Charlie, please --

                    LUCKY
          I'll be fine. I mean it.

He touches her face. A brief, gentle gesture -- his hand on her cheek for just a moment.
Then he turns to Hernandez.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Let's go, chief. No need to make a
          scene in a nice restaurant.

Hernandez steps aside. Lucky walks between the officers toward the door. The restaurant watches
in silence. Every eye. Every fork set down.

EXT. EL JARDIN RESTAURANT - CONTINUOUS

Lucky steps through the front entrance into the afternoon sun. The flashbulbs explode --
PHOTOGRAPHERS pressing forward, cameras clicking, the mechanical rattle of shutters and film
advancing.

Lucky doesn't flinch. Doesn't shield his face. Doesn't lower his head. He walks straight,
looking directly ahead, the way a man walks when he knows the pictures will be in every
newspaper tomorrow.

The officers guide him toward the police car. One opens the rear door.

Lucky pauses. Looks back at the restaurant. Through the window, he can see Igea at the table,
her head in her hands.

LUCKY (V.O.)
          I'm having lunch in a restaurant. It was
          a Saturday afternoon. I think it was
          February 23, and the assistant police chief,
          Hernandez, come up to my table and arrested
          me.

He ducks into the car. The door closes.

Delgado stands by Lucky's sedan, watching. The police car pulls away. The photographers lower
their cameras. One of them is smiling -- he got the shot.

Delgado opens the sedan door. Sits behind the wheel. Grips the steering wheel with both hands
and doesn't start the engine.

Through the restaurant window, Igea is still at the table. A waiter approaches, uncertain.
She waves him away. The wine glasses sit untouched, the food going cold.

The lunch Lucky will never finish. The Saturday that ended everything.

Delgado starts the car and pulls away from the curb. Slowly. Like a man driving away from
something he helped destroy.


----------------------------------------
# Scene 22 - Lansky's Visit
----------------------------------------

EXT. TISCORNIA IMMIGRATION CAMP - DAY (LATE FEBRUARY 1947)

SUPER: TISCORNIA DETENTION CAMP -- LATE FEBRUARY 1947

A bleak compound on the edge of Havana harbor. Low concrete buildings. Chain-link fencing
topped with barbed wire. Guard towers. The Caribbean sun beats down on everything with the same
indifferent beauty it shows the mansions of Miramar.

A far cry from the Hotel Nacional. A far cry from anywhere Lucky Luciano has been in five
months.

LUCKY LUCIANO stands on his side of the fence in a plain shirt, no jacket, no tie. The gray
suit from El Jardin is gone. He looks thinner. Older. The composure is intact but the setting
has stripped away everything that supported it.

A car pulls up on the other side of the fence. The door opens.

MEYER LANSKY steps out. He's dressed in his usual linen suit, but his face carries a weight
that wasn't there when he greeted Lucky on the tarmac five months ago.

He walks to the fence. Lucky walks to his side of it.

Two men who built an empire together, separated by chain link.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie.

                    LUCKY
          Meyer.

A beat. The same greeting as the airport. Different world.

                    LANSKY
          How are they treating you?

                    LUCKY
          Like a man waiting for a boat. They feed
          me. I sleep. I wait.

                    LANSKY
          I brought some things. Books, cigarettes.
          They'll let them through.

                    LUCKY
          You're a good friend, Meyer.

                    LANSKY
          I'm your only friend.

Lucky almost smiles. Almost.

                    LANSKY (CONT'D)
          We need to talk about the money.

                    LUCKY
          Talk.

Lansky glances around. A GUARD stands thirty feet away, bored, not listening.

                    LANSKY
          The Hotel Nacional accounts are clean. I've
          moved your share to the numbered accounts
          in Switzerland. The racetrack lease is void --
          they cancelled it the day after the arrest.
          The airline is frozen. Cuban government
          seized the operating license.

                    LUCKY
          The mansion?

                    LANSKY
          Back to Perez Damera. Your personal things
          are in storage. I'll ship them to Naples.

Lucky nods. The inventory of a kingdom being dismantled, item by item. Accounts, leases,
licenses. A life reduced to logistics.

                    LUCKY
          And the other business?

Lansky understands. The French Connection. The pipeline.

                    LANSKY
          Still running. The Corsicans don't care
          who sits in what chair -- they care about
          the product moving. That won't stop.

                    LUCKY
          Good. Don't let Vito near it.

Lansky's expression changes. Something harder. Something afraid.

                    LANSKY
          That's what I came to talk about.

                    LUCKY
          Vito.

                    LANSKY
          He's already moving. The day after your
          arrest, he called a meeting in New York.
          Costello, Adonis, Moretti -- everyone.
          He's telling them the old arrangement
          is over. He's telling them he's in charge.

                    LUCKY
          Nobody agreed to that.

                    LANSKY
          Nobody had to. You're behind a fence in
          Cuba, Charlie. Who's going to stop him?
          Costello? Moretti? They're businessmen,
          not fighters.

                    LUCKY
          Albert.

                    LANSKY
          Albert is the only one with the guts to
          stand up to him. And that's what worries
          me.

A silence. Both men know what Lansky means. Anastasia's loyalty is his greatest quality and
his greatest vulnerability. Genovese won't tolerate a man who declared "For me, you are the
Big Boss" in front of every family head in America.

                    LUCKY
          You think Vito will move on Albert.

                    LANSKY
          I think Vito will move on everyone who
          stood with you. Albert first. Then Costello.
          Then anyone who reminds him that he lost
          that vote.

                    LUCKY
          And you?

                    LANSKY
          I'm useful. I know where the money is.
          That buys time. Not forever.

Lucky steps closer to the fence. His fingers curl around the chain link.

                    LUCKY
          Meyer. Listen to me. Warn Albert. Warn
          Costello. Tell them what's coming. And you --
          you keep your head down. Stay in Florida.
          Stay useful. Stay alive.

                    LANSKY
          And you?

                    LUCKY
          I'll be in Naples. An old man with no
          country. What's Vito going to do, send
          someone to Italy to kill me? I'm already
          dead to them.

                    LANSKY
          Don't say that.

                    LUCKY
          It's not self-pity, Meyer. It's geography.

Another silence. The guard shifts his weight, yawns. The sun moves behind a cloud and the light
changes -- the compound goes gray for a moment.

                    LANSKY
          Do you remember when we were kids? Delancey
          Street? You said to me -- I must have been,
          what, fourteen -- you said, "Meyer, the
          trick isn't having the power. The trick is
          keeping it."

                    LUCKY
          I remember.

                    LANSKY
          You were right. You were always right
          about that. And we kept it for thirty years.
          That's more than most.

                    LUCKY
          It wasn't enough.

Lansky puts his hand on the fence. Lucky puts his hand on the other side. Their fingers don't
quite touch through the chain link. Two old friends. One behind a fence. The partnership that
built an empire, winding down.

                    LANSKY
          I'll keep things running from my end.
          Whatever I can. The Cuba operation is
          finished, but the rest -- I'll hold
          what I can hold.

                    LUCKY
          I know you will.

                    LANSKY
          Charlie.

                    LUCKY
          Yeah.

                    LANSKY
          It was a hell of a run.

Lucky looks at Lansky through the fence. The man who told him Cuba was wide open. The man who
met him at the airport. The man who said "It's everything" as they drove through Havana for the
first time.

                    LUCKY
          It was a hell of a run.

Lansky nods. Steps back from the fence. Straightens his jacket. Turns toward the car.

He stops. Turns back.

                    LANSKY
          I'll see you in Naples.

                    LUCKY
          I'll be there.

Lansky walks to the car. Opens the door. Gets in. The engine starts.

Lucky watches the car pull away through the chain-link fence. His hand stays on the wire until
the car turns the corner and disappears.

He stands alone in the yard. The sun comes back. The Caribbean light falls on the detention
camp the same way it falls on the mansions of Miramar, the casinos of the Nacional, the
restaurants of the Vedado -- beautiful, indifferent, constant.

Lucky turns and walks back inside.

# Scene 23 - Genovese's Visit
----------------------------------------

EXT. TISCORNIA IMMIGRATION CAMP - DAY (EARLY MARCH 1947)

SUPER: TISCORNIA DETENTION CAMP -- EARLY MARCH 1947

The same fence. The same compound. The same Caribbean sun. But this visit is different.

A car pulls up -- newer, shinier than Lansky's. The door opens and VITO GENOVESE steps out.
Dark suit, dark tie, dark eyes. He moves like a man who owns the ground he's walking on.

He adjusts his cuffs. Looks at the detention camp the way a man looks at a house he's
considering buying. Amused. Satisfied.

A GUARD opens the gate to the visiting area. Genovese walks through.

LUCKY LUCIANO is already at the fence. He's been told who's coming. His posture is different
from the Lansky visit -- straighter, harder. His hands hang at his sides. He doesn't touch
the fence.

Genovese approaches. Stops three feet from the chain link. Smiles.

                    GENOVESE
          Charlie. Look at you.

                    LUCKY
          Vito.

                    GENOVESE
          They got you in a cage. Like an animal.

                    LUCKY
          I'm fine.

                    GENOVESE
          No. You're not fine. You're in a detention
          camp on a rock in the Caribbean waiting for
          a cargo ship to take you back to Italy.
          That's not fine, Charlie. That's finished.

Lucky says nothing. He watches Genovese the way he watched him at the conference -- reading
the man, measuring the threat. But the calculus is different now. At the conference, Lucky held
the power. Now Genovese does.

                    GENOVESE (CONT'D)
          I came to pay my respects.

                    LUCKY
          You came to gloat.

                    GENOVESE
          Maybe a little of both.

Genovese reaches into his jacket. Produces a cigar. Lights it slowly, taking his time. The
flame reflected in his eyes. He doesn't offer one to Lucky.

                    GENOVESE (CONT'D)
          You want to know how it happened? The
          arrest, the ultimatum, the whole thing?
          You want to know how the great Lucky
          Luciano ended up behind a fence?

                    LUCKY
          I know how it happened. The Americans
          threatened to cut medical supplies.
          Batista folded.

                    GENOVESE
          That's the what. I'm asking if you
          want to know the who.

A beat. Lucky's eyes narrow.

                    LUCKY
          Talk.

Genovese takes a long drag on the cigar. Blows the smoke toward the fence. It drifts through
the chain link toward Lucky.

                    GENOVESE
          The FBN. The Federal Bureau of Narcotics.
          You know about them. They had a man in
          Havana -- some agent, nobody important.
          He was running around the Nacional with
          his informants and his little notebook,
          putting together a case.

                    LUCKY
          I know about the agent.

                    GENOVESE
          Good. Here's what you don't know. Every
          piece of intelligence that agent collected?
          His bosses in Washington already had it.
          Every name from the conference, every route,
          every dollar amount. They had it before he
          reported it.

Lucky stares at Genovese. The pieces starting to assemble.

                    LUCKY
          Someone was feeding them.

                    GENOVESE
          Someone was feeding them everything.

A silence. Then it lands.

                    LUCKY
          You.

Genovese smiles. Wide. The smile of a man who has been waiting months to reveal this.

                    GENOVESE
          Me.

                    LUCKY
          You fed the FBN.

                    GENOVESE
          Every detail. Conference attendees,
          heroin routes, distribution territories,
          your Cuba investments -- all of it. I
          picked up the phone and I gave them
          everything they needed to bury you.

                    LUCKY
          You used the United States government
          to do your dirty work.

                    GENOVESE
          I used them like a gun, Charlie. I pointed
          them at you and I pulled the trigger. And
          the beautiful part? They think they did it
          themselves. That agent thinks he's a hero.
          His director got his career-making case.
          Everybody won. Except you.

Lucky's hands curl into fists at his sides. But he doesn't move. Doesn't shout. The rage is
there -- deep, cold, volcanic -- but he holds it. Because losing control in front of Genovese
is the last thing he can afford.

                    LUCKY
          You lost the vote, Vito. You stood in front
          of every boss in America and you lost. Albert
          stood up and put you in your place. And
          instead of taking it like a man, you ran to
          the feds.

                    GENOVESE
          I took it like a smart man. A vote is one
          day. This is forever.

                    LUCKY
          This is treason. You went to the feds.
          Every man in our thing would put you in
          the ground for that.

                    GENOVESE
          Who's going to tell them? You?
               (gestures at the fence)
          From where?

Lucky says nothing. Genovese has him and they both know it.

                    GENOVESE (CONT'D)
          Now. Let's talk about the future. The
          French Connection. The pipeline. Turkey,
          Sicily, France, Cuba, the ports. I want it.

                    LUCKY
          No.

                    GENOVESE
          No?

                    LUCKY
          You're not getting the pipeline. The
          Corsicans work with me. The Sicilians
          work with me. You don't have those
          relationships.

                    GENOVESE
          I'll build new ones.

                    LUCKY
          You'll destroy what took years to build.

                    GENOVESE
          Then I'll build something bigger. You had
          limits, Charlie. You had your code, your
          rules, your gentleman's agreements. You
          know what I have? Nothing holding me back.
               (beat)
          You projected fifty million a year from
          the heroin route. Fifty million. You know
          what I can do without your limits? Without
          your restraint?

                    LUCKY
          You'll flood the streets.

                    GENOVESE
          I'll flood the country. Every city, every
          neighborhood, every corner. And when the
          money starts coming in -- real money, not
          your careful little fifty million -- nobody
          is going to care how it got there.

                    LUCKY
          You think you're building an empire. You're
          building a bomb. It'll blow up in your face,
          Vito. The feds will come for you the same
          way they came for me.

                    GENOVESE
          Maybe. But not today. Today, I'm on this
          side of the fence. And you're on that side.

Genovese drops his cigar on the ground. Grinds it out with his shoe.

                    GENOVESE (CONT'D)
          Goodbye, Charlie. Have a nice trip.
          I hear Italy's beautiful this time of year.

He turns and walks toward the gate. Slow, deliberate. A man savoring the walk.

                    LUCKY
          Vito.

Genovese stops. Turns halfway.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Albert stood up for me. In front of
          everyone. He said I was the boss whether
          I liked it or not. You remember?

                    GENOVESE
          I remember.

                    LUCKY
          Leave him alone.

Genovese holds Lucky's gaze for a long moment. Then that smile again -- cold, thin, the smile
of a man who has already made his plans.

                    GENOVESE
          I'll give Albert your regards.

He turns and walks through the gate. The guard locks it behind him. Genovese gets into his car.
The engine starts. The car pulls away.

Lucky stands at the fence. Alone. His hands grip the chain link, knuckles white.

He knows. He knows what Genovese will do. He knows what will happen to Anastasia, to Costello,
to the heroin trade, to everything.

He was outmaneuvered. Not by the FBN, not by the Cuban government, not by the American
ultimatum. By Vito Genovese. The man who smiled at the conference, who shook his hand when
ordered to, who played the long game while everyone else played the short one.

Lucky releases the fence. Flexes his fingers. Turns and walks toward the barracks.

He has a message to send. To Lansky. To Anastasia. They're all targets now.

# Scene 24 - The Twist
----------------------------------------

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - DAY (MID-MARCH 1947)

SUPER: MID-MARCH 1947

A modest hotel room. Not the suites upstairs where the mob bosses gathered in December -- a
small room on a lower floor, the kind a tourist or businessman would take. JACK BRENNAN sits on
the bed, staring at a telegram in his hand.

CLOSE ON the telegram: "MISSION ACCOMPLISHED STOP LUCIANO DEPORTATION CONFIRMED STOP
OUTSTANDING WORK JACK STOP RETURN WASHINGTON SOONEST STOP -- HARTLEY"

Mission accomplished. The words he should be celebrating.

Brennan sets the telegram on the nightstand. He looks at it the way a man looks at a letter
he doesn't trust. He opens the drawer, pulls out a bottle of rum, pours two fingers into a
water glass. Drinks.

He sits at the small desk. In front of him: his case file. Stacks of notes, reports, dates,
names. Months of work. He opens it and starts leafing through pages.

He stops on a report. His own handwritten notes from January -- the anomaly he noticed.
Washington seemed to already know certain intelligence before he reported it. The conference
attendees. The heroin routes. Specific dollar amounts.

He pulls out another page. A decoded telegram from Hartley, dated early February. Instructions
that assumed knowledge Brennan hadn't yet transmitted.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          I kept coming back to the same question.
          How did Washington know things before I
          told them? I assumed other sources. Other
          agents. Diplomatic channels.

He spreads the pages on the desk. A timeline taking shape. His reports. Hartley's responses.
The gaps where information appeared in Washington before Brennan sent it.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          I assumed wrong.

He pulls out more pages. Lays them side by side. His report on the conference attendees --
dated January 8th. Hartley's response -- dated January 7th. Hartley already had the list
before Brennan sent it.

His report on heroin routes and revenue projections -- January 12th. Hartley's telegram
referencing "the fifty million figure" -- January 10th. Two days before Brennan reported it.

Brennan stares at the dates. Then he pulls out his notes from the second phone call with
Hartley. The one where he raised concerns about Genovese. He reads his own handwriting:
"Hartley shut it down immediately. 'Genovese is not your assignment.' Why?"

He picks up the photograph of Genovese -- the one he put facedown on the desk in January.
Turns it over. Studies the face.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          Genovese challenged Lucky at the conference.
          Lost the vote. Got humiliated in front of
          twenty bosses. Two days later, someone tried
          to kill Lucky at his mansion. New York plates.
          I reported it. Hartley wasn't interested.

He pins the photograph to his corkboard. Draws a line from Genovese to Washington.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          I told Hartley that Genovese was more
          dangerous than Luciano. He said stay focused.
          I told him Genovese was making moves. He
          said stay focused. Every time I pulled at
          the Genovese thread, Hartley cut it.

He sits back. Looks at the corkboard. The web of connections. Lucky at the center, where
Brennan put him months ago. But now a new line runs from Genovese to a word Brennan writes
in block letters and pins above everything else:

FBN.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          Not other agents. Not diplomatic
          channels. Genovese. Vito Genovese was
          feeding Hartley intelligence on Lucky's
          operations. The conference details, the
          heroin routes, the financial numbers --
          Hartley had all of it before I reported
          a word. Because a mob underboss was handing
          it to him on a plate.

Brennan leans forward. His hands are flat on the desk. His breathing is controlled.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          And Hartley knew. He knew his source
          was the man trying to overthrow Lucky.
          He knew Genovese was using the Bureau
          as his weapon. And he took it anyway
          because it gave him the biggest case
          of his career.

Brennan stands. Walks to the window. Looks out at Havana -- the city he came to save from
a drug lord. The city where he was used.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          Delgado told me. Months ago. He said I
          was creating a bigger monster. I didn't
          listen.

He turns from the window. Picks up the phone.

                    BRENNAN
          Operator. I need a long-distance line
          to Washington, D.C.

Static. Clicks. The hum of an international connection.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Brennan. I was just going to call. When
          are you flying back?

                    BRENNAN
          We need to talk, sir.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Of course. Come to the office when you're
          back and we'll --

                    BRENNAN
          Now. We need to talk now.

A pause. Hartley reads the tone.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          All right, Jack. What's on your mind?

                    BRENNAN
          Genovese.

Silence on the line.

                    BRENNAN (CONT'D)
          Vito Genovese has been feeding you
          intelligence on Lucky's operations for
          months. Before I even got to Havana.
          Every conference detail, every heroin
          route, every number. You already had
          it all. From a mob underboss who was
          engineering a coup against his boss.

A long pause. The line hums.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Where is this coming from, Jack?

                    BRENNAN
          Your telegrams. Your responses to my
          reports. You referenced the fifty million
          figure two days before I sent it. You had
          the attendee list before I filed it. And
          every time I flagged Genovese, you shut
          me down. Because he was your source. And
          you couldn't have me pulling at your
          source.

Another pause. When Hartley speaks, his voice is flat. Careful. The voice of a man who has
already decided how much to admit.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Jack, intelligence comes from many
          sources. Some of them are unsavory.
          That's the nature of --

                    BRENNAN
          Is it true?

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          The Bureau uses whatever information
          is available to --

                    BRENNAN
          Did you know your source was Vito Genovese?
          Yes or no.

The longest pause yet. Then:

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Yes.

Brennan closes his eyes. His hand tightens on the receiver.

                    BRENNAN
          You knew. You knew a mob underboss was
          feeding you intelligence to eliminate his
          rival. You knew he was using the FBN as
          his personal weapon. And you took the
          information anyway.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          I took actionable intelligence that
          resulted in the deportation of the most
          dangerous mob boss in American history.
          That is my job, Jack.

                    BRENNAN
          We didn't take down Lucky. Genovese did.
          We were his muscle.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          And the result is the same.

                    BRENNAN
          The result is not the same! Genovese is
          taking over. He's seizing the heroin
          network. He's going to expand it -- no
          restraint, no limits. The pipeline we
          documented? He's going to turn it into
          a flood. Every city in America.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Luciano is gone. That's what matters.
          That's what your brother would have wanted.

Brennan pulls the receiver away from his ear. Stares at it. His brother. Hartley is invoking
his dead brother.

                    BRENNAN
          Don't you talk about my brother. My
          brother died from heroin. And the man
          who's about to control all the heroin
          in America is the man you just helped
          put in charge.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Jack --

                    BRENNAN
          I'm filing a report. Everything.
          Genovese's role as your source, the
          compromised intelligence, the power
          vacuum we've created. All of it.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          You can file whatever you want, Jack.
          It won't matter. Luciano is deported.
          The case is closed. The Bureau has its
          win. Nobody in Washington wants to hear
          that the win was engineered by another
          mobster. Nobody wants that story.

                    BRENNAN
          Then they should.

                    HARTLEY (O.S.)
          Come home, Jack. Write your report.
          Take a vacation. And accept that
          sometimes the way things get done isn't
          the way you learned about in training.

The line goes dead. Hartley has hung up.

Brennan holds the receiver. The dial tone buzzes. He sets it down.

He sits at the desk. Opens his case file. Picks up a pen.

He starts to write. The report. Genovese's role. Hartley's complicity. The compromised
intelligence. The power vacuum. The coming storm.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          I filed the report. Forty-seven pages.
          Every source, every date, every connection.
          It was received, stamped, and filed. Nobody
          read it. Nobody acted on it. The Bureau had
          its win. The story was over.

He writes. The pen scratches across paper. The Havana light slants through the window and falls
across the desk, across the pages, across the hands of a man documenting a truth that no one
wants to hear.

TIME CUT:

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - THE NEXT MORNING

Brennan is packing his suitcase. The corkboard is bare -- he's taken everything down, packed
the notes and photographs into his case file. The room looks like it did when he checked in.
James Burke, textile buyer from Philadelphia, checking out.

A KNOCK at the door. Brennan opens it. A BELLBOY holds a telegram.

                    BELLBOY
          Senor Burke. For you.

Brennan takes it. Tips the boy. Closes the door.

He opens the telegram. Reads it. His face changes -- not shock, not anger. Something worse.
Resignation.

CLOSE ON the telegram: "RE: NEXT ASSIGNMENT STOP REPORT MEXICO CITY STATION APRIL 1 STOP
NEW TARGET NEW OPERATION STOP DETAILS ON ARRIVAL STOP EXPECT FULL BRIEFING STOP -- HARTLEY"

Brennan sits on the bed. Holds the telegram in both hands. Reads it again.

Another city. Another target. Another operation where he won't know who's really pulling the
strings until it's too late. The machine doesn't stop. It doesn't reflect. It doesn't learn.
It just moves him to the next square on the board.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.)
          Mexico City. A new target. A new
          operation. New informants to recruit,
          new evidence to gather, new reports
          to file that nobody would read.

He sets the telegram on the bed. Stares at the wall.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          And I kept asking myself the same
          questions. What country am I in?
          Who are we fighting? Who is the
          bad guy?

He picks up his suitcase. Looks around the empty room one last time. The room where he
built a case, lost his certainty, and learned that the institution he served would use
him the same way a mobster would -- as a weapon pointed at whoever was convenient.

                    BRENNAN (V.O.) (CONT'D)
          I never did get an answer.

He walks out. The door closes behind him.

The room is empty. The Havana light falls on bare walls and a stripped bed. On the
nightstand, the first telegram -- MISSION ACCOMPLISHED -- sits where he left it. He
didn't take it with him.

# Scene 25 - Delgado's Goodbye
----------------------------------------

EXT. TISCORNIA IMMIGRATION CAMP - LATE AFTERNOON (MID-MARCH 1947)

SUPER: TISCORNIA DETENTION CAMP -- MID-MARCH 1947

Late afternoon. The sun is lower now, the light softer than during the previous visits.
The compound looks almost gentle in the golden hour -- the concrete less harsh, the fence
less sharp. Almost.

CARLOS DELGADO walks toward the visiting fence. He's in his dark suit, clean-shaven, the same
military bearing as the day he was assigned to Lucky's detail at the airport. But he moves
differently now -- slower, carrying something invisible.

He reaches the fence. Waits.

LUCKY LUCIANO emerges from the barracks. He walks to the fence. Sees Delgado.

A beat. The two men look at each other through the chain link. The bodyguard and the man he was
assigned to protect. The cop and the criminal. The betrayer and the forgiven.

                    LUCKY
          Carlos.

                    DELGADO
          Senor Luciano.

                    LUCKY
          Five months and you're still calling
          me that.

                    DELGADO
          It is your name.

                    LUCKY
          My name is Charlie. My friends call
          me Charlie.

A silence. Delgado absorbs the word. Friends. After everything.

                    DELGADO
          I came to --
               (stops)
          I don't know what I came to say.

                    LUCKY
          Then don't say anything. Just stand
          there. It's good to see a familiar face.

They stand in silence for a moment. The yard is mostly empty. A few other detainees in the
distance. A guard leaning against a wall, smoking.

                    DELGADO
          I gave them your schedule. Saturday lunch.
          El Jardin. One o'clock. The table near the
          garden.

                    LUCKY
          I know.

                    DELGADO
          You know?

                    LUCKY
          Carlos. You drove me every Saturday for
          five months. You knew my routine better
          than I did. When the arrest happened exactly
          where I always sit, at exactly the time I
          always arrive -- who else would it be?

Delgado's face tightens. He expected anger. Expected Lucky to look at him the way Genovese
would -- with vengeance, with calculation, with the cold arithmetic of betrayal.

Instead, Lucky shrugs.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          You did your job. That's more than I can
          say for most of the people I've dealt with
          on this island.

                    DELGADO
          It was not just my job.

                    LUCKY
          No?

                    DELGADO
          I believed it was the right thing.
          Brennan said -- the heroin, the network,
          the damage it would do. I believed him.
          I believed we were stopping something
          terrible.

                    LUCKY
          And now?

Delgado looks at the ground. Then back at Lucky.

                    DELGADO
          Now I am not sure. I told Brennan that
          removing you might create something worse.
          I told him about Genovese. I told him that
          the man who challenged you at the conference
          has no limits.

                    LUCKY
          You were right.

                    DELGADO
          I didn't want to be right.

Another silence. The sun moves lower. The shadows of the fence posts lengthen across the dirt
yard.

                    LUCKY
          You got a good family, Carlos. Your wife,
          your two girls. I remember you told me
          their names once. I forget now.

                    DELGADO
          Ana and Sofia.

                    LUCKY
          Ana and Sofia. Go home to Ana and
          Sofia. Forget about all of this.

                    DELGADO
          Can I forget?

                    LUCKY
          Probably not. But go home anyway.

Lucky steps closer to the fence. His voice drops. Not conspiratorial -- intimate. The voice of
one man offering another something honest.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Carlos. You were the only honest man
          in this whole arrangement. Valdes took
          his envelopes. The police took their
          orders. The government took the Americans'
          money and then folded to the Americans'
          threats. Your FBN friend was being used
          by people he trusted.
               (beat)
          You were the only one who did what he
          did because he believed it was right.
          That counts for something. Even to a
          man like me.

Delgado's eyes are bright. He blinks. Looks away.

                    DELGADO
          You are not what I expected.

                    LUCKY
          What did you expect?

                    DELGADO
          A monster.

                    LUCKY
          Maybe I am one. But there are worse
          monsters. You know that now.

Delgado nods. Slowly.

                    DELGADO
          The ship leaves in a few days. The Bakir.
          A Turkish cargo freighter.

                    LUCKY
          I heard. Not exactly the Queen Mary.

                    DELGADO
          I am sorry it ends this way.

                    LUCKY
          It was always going to end some way.
          This way is better than most.

Lucky extends his hand through a gap in the chain link. Delgado reaches through and takes it.
They shake -- a firm, genuine grip. The same handshake as the airport lounge five months ago,
when Lucky sized up the Secret Police officer with the military bearing and the careful eyes.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Take care of yourself, Carlos.

                    DELGADO
          And you, Senor --
               (catches himself)
          And you, Charlie.

Lucky almost smiles. He releases Delgado's hand.

Delgado steps back from the fence. He straightens his jacket -- the old habit, the professional
reflex of a man who has spent five months keeping his appearance sharp for a boss who noticed
everything.

He turns and walks toward the gate. His footsteps are steady. He doesn't look back.

Lucky watches him go. The last of his three visitors. Lansky, the friend. Genovese, the enemy.
Delgado -- the man who was both, and neither.

The gate opens. Delgado walks through. The gate closes.

Lucky stands at the fence in the late afternoon light. Alone. The sun touches the top of the
concrete wall and the compound fills with amber.

He turns and walks back to the barracks. A few more days. Then the ship. Then Italy. Then
whatever comes next for a man with an empire and no country to run it from.

# Scene 26 - The Deportation
----------------------------------------

EXT. HAVANA HARBOR - NIGHT (MARCH 19, 1947)

SUPER: HAVANA HARBOR -- MARCH 19, 1947

Night. The harbor is dark except for dock lights casting yellow pools on the concrete pier.
The water is black, restless, slapping against the pilings.

The S.S. BAKIR sits at the dock -- a Turkish cargo freighter. Old, rust-streaked, the paint
peeling from her hull. She is nothing like the S.S. Laura Keene that carried Lucky out of
New York a year ago. She is a ship for cargo and deportees, not passengers and promises.

The gangway is down. Crew members move in the shadows, preparing for departure.

A police escort arrives. Two cars. The first carries LUCKY LUCIANO, flanked by OFFICERS. The
second carries his luggage -- two suitcases. Not much for a man who owned a mansion.

Lucky steps out of the car. He's in a dark overcoat, collar up against the harbor wind. He
looks at the ship. Takes it in. The rust, the size, the indignity of it.

He doesn't complain. He never complains.

IGEA LISSONI is waiting on the pier. She's been crying -- her eyes are red, her face drawn.
She wears a dark coat, clutching it around herself. When she sees Lucky, she moves toward him.

The officers step aside. Even they understand that this moment belongs to the two of them.

Igea reaches Lucky. She takes his hands.

                    IGEA
          Charlie.

                    LUCKY
          Hey.

                    IGEA
          I tried to get them to let me come
          with you. They said no.

                    LUCKY
          I know.

                    IGEA
          Twenty-three days on that ship. Alone.

                    LUCKY
          I've been alone before. I'll manage.

She presses her forehead against his chest. His arms go around her. He holds her -- tightly,
the way a man holds something he's losing.

                    IGEA
          When will I see you?

                    LUCKY
          Come to Naples. When things settle down,
          come to Naples. I'll be there.

                    IGEA
          Promise me.

                    LUCKY
          I promise.

She pulls back. Looks at his face. Touches it -- her palm against his cheek, memorizing the
feel of it.

                    LUCKY (CONT'D)
          Don't cry on the dock. Not in front
          of them.

She nods. Wipes her eyes. Straightens.

                    IGEA
          I'm not crying.

                    LUCKY
          Good.

He kisses her. Brief. Firm. Then releases her.

AT THE EDGE OF THE PIER -- fifty yards away, in the shadows beyond the dock lights:

JACK BRENNAN leans against a warehouse wall. Watching. His hands are in his pockets. He's
alone. No telegram to send, no report to file. Just a man watching the result of his work.

This should be his victory. The mission accomplished. The man deported. Instead, Brennan
watches with the hollow eyes of a man who knows that what he's seeing is not an ending but
a beginning -- the beginning of something worse.

BACK ON THE PIER:

An OFFICER approaches Lucky.

                    OFFICER
          Senor Luciano. It is time.

Lucky nods. He picks up one of his suitcases. An officer takes the other.

He walks toward the gangway. Stops at the bottom. Turns and looks back at Havana.

The city spreads beyond the harbor -- lights twinkling in the hills of Vedado, the glow of the
Malecon, the distant silhouette of the Hotel Nacional on its bluff. The city that was his
kingdom for one hundred and forty-one days.

Lucky looks at Igea. She stands where he left her, arms wrapped around herself.

He looks at the city one more time.

                    LUCKY
          I'll be back when the current government
          is gone.

He turns and walks up the gangway. His shoes ring on the metal steps. The officers wait below.
The ship's crew takes his suitcase.

Lucky reaches the deck. He moves to the railing and looks down at the pier. At Igea. At the
officers. At the harbor and the city beyond.

A HORN sounds. Deep, low, echoing across the water. The crew begins to cast off lines. The
gangway is pulled away.

The S.S. Bakir shudders. The engines engage. Slowly -- agonizingly slowly -- the ship begins
to pull away from the pier.

Igea watches from the dock. She raises one hand. Lucky raises his.

The gap between the ship and the pier widens. Ten feet. Twenty. Fifty.

ON THE PIER:

Brennan watches the ship pull away. The doubt in his eyes has hardened into something else --
certainty. The certainty that he helped do the wrong thing for the right reasons, and the
consequences will be paid by people who had nothing to do with any of it.

He turns and walks away from the harbor. Into the dark streets of Havana. Toward the airport.
Toward Washington. Toward the report that no one will read.

INT. DELGADO HOME - LIVING ROOM - NIGHT

A small, warm home. Modest furniture. Family photographs on the wall. The sounds of a Havana
night drifting through open windows -- music, voices, the city that goes on living.

CARLOS DELGADO walks through the front door. He removes his jacket. Hangs it on a hook.

MARIA DELGADO emerges from the kitchen. She reads his face and says nothing. She's learned
not to ask about certain days.

Two YOUNG GIRLS -- ANA and SOFIA, Delgado's daughters -- run from the back room.

                    ANA
          Papa!

                    SOFIA
          Papa, you're home!

Delgado kneels. Catches them both. Pulls them into his arms and holds them -- fiercely, as if
the world outside might take them too.

                    DELGADO
          I'm home.

Maria watches from the doorway. She knows. Not the details -- never the details. But she knows
that something ended today and that her husband is carrying the weight of it.

Delgado lifts his daughters. One in each arm. Carries them toward the back of the house. Maria
follows.

The front door stays open. Through it, the sounds of Havana at night. The city doesn't know
that Lucky Luciano just sailed away on a cargo ship. The city doesn't know what comes next.
Nobody does.

EXT. HAVANA HARBOR - NIGHT

The S.S. Bakir moves through the harbor mouth into open water. Her lights grow smaller. The
rusty hull vanishes into the darkness of the Straits of Florida.

LUCKY stands at the stern railing. Havana's lights are a line on the horizon, shrinking.

Ninety miles that way is Florida. The country that exiled him. The country that followed him
to Cuba. The country that used its hospitals and its leverage and its Bureau to push him out
one more time.

The lights disappear. The ocean is black in every direction.

Lucky turns from the railing. Walks toward the hatch. Twenty-three days to Italy. To Naples.
To exile.

The exile king, exiled again.

FADE OUT.

TITLE CARD: "APRIL 11, 1947 -- GENOA, ITALY"

The S.S. Bakir limps into port. Twenty-three days at sea. LUCKY LUCIANO descends the gangway

Lucky walks alone through a Naples street. An old man in an overcoat, collar up, passing cafes

Crates loaded onto a cargo ship. MEN in work clothes move with purpose. Coded labels. A

GUNFIRE. The mirrors shatter. Anastasia falls from the chair.

# Scene 28 - Final Image

SPLIT SCREEN - THREE MEN, THREE TIMES

FADE IN FROM BLACK.

The screen divides into three panels. Three images. Three moments. Three men shaped by five
months in Havana.

LEFT PANEL -- 1947:

EXT. S.S. BAKIR - STERN DECK - NIGHT (1947)

LUCKY LUCIANO stands at the railing of the cargo ship. The wind off the Straits of Florida
catches his overcoat. Behind him, the lights of Havana are gone. There is only the dark ocean.

He grips the railing. The exile king, sailing into exile. The man who built an empire ninety
miles from Florida, dismantled by forces he couldn't see and allies he couldn't trust.

His face is still. Composed. The dignity that even a rusty cargo freighter cannot take from
him. But his eyes -- his eyes hold the knowledge of a man who understands, finally, that he
was outplayed.

CENTER PANEL -- 1950s:

INT. GENOVESE'S OFFICE - NIGHT (1950s)

VITO GENOVESE sits behind a desk in a dark-paneled room. The desk is large, expensive. A map
of the United States on the wall behind him, pins marking cities. Every pin a distribution
point. Every city a market.

He smokes a cigar. The same deliberate enjoyment as the detention camp fence. The man who lost
the vote at the Havana Conference won the war. He used the FBN, manipulated a director,
weaponized an agent, and seized everything.

No restraint. No code. No limits.

He leans back in his chair. The king of heroin. The bigger monster. The man Delgado warned
about and Brennan didn't listen.

RIGHT PANEL -- 1959:

EXT. HAVANA STREETS - DAY (1959)

CARLOS DELGADO stands on a Havana sidewalk. The revolution streams past him -- crowds, flags,
soldiers, the euphoria of a nation transformed. Castro's new Cuba.

Delgado watches. He is older now. Gray at the temples. The military bearing softened by twelve
years. Beside him, MARIA holds his arm. Their daughters are grown, somewhere in the crowd, part
of the new Cuba.

He watches the revolution the way a man watches a river change course. The Cuba he served --
Batista's Cuba, the Cuba of envelopes and arrangements and police lookaway -- is gone. The
casinos shuttered. The mansions seized. The men like Valdes and Perez Damera swept away.

He tried to do the right thing. He protected Lucky, then helped bring him down. He chose his
country over his conscience and his conscience over his comfort.

The country chose something else entirely.

THE THREE PANELS HOLD.

Three men. Three paths. Each one believing he understood the game. Each one wrong.

Lucky, who believed Cuba was beyond America's reach. Genovese, who believed power without
restraint was strength. Delgado, who believed that doing the right thing would make things
right.

THE PANELS SLOWLY MERGE TO BLACK.

One by one, the images darken. Genovese first -- his empire will end in a prison cell. Then
Lucky -- his exile will end on a park bench in Naples, a hand clutching his chest. Delgado
last -- the man who survived, who held his daughters, who lived to watch his country transform
and transform again.

BLACK SCREEN.

TITLE CARD:

"Two men tried to do the right thing.

But sometimes doing the right thing just changes which evil is in power."

The title holds. Five seconds. Ten.

TITLE CARD:

"Lucky Luciano spent 141 days in Cuba, from October 29, 1946 to March 19, 1947.

In those five months, the future of organized crime in America was decided."

TITLE CARD:

"The French Connection operated for twenty-five years.

Over eighty percent of heroin entering the United States flowed through the network
Lucky built and Genovese expanded."

TITLE CARD:

"Albert Anastasia -- who defended Lucky at the Havana Conference -- was assassinated
in 1957 on Genovese's orders.

The price of loyalty, paid ten years later."

BLACK SCREEN.

Silence.

FADE OUT.
