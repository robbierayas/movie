# Scene 18 - The Ultimatum

## LOCATION
INT. - FBN HEADQUARTERS, WASHINGTON D.C. / STATE DEPARTMENT - DAY (February 10-21, 1947)

## CHARACTERS
Director Hartley, Jack Brennan (via phone), U.S. State Department Officials, President Batista

## SCENE LAYOUT

### Opening
The Act 2 climax. In Washington, Director Hartley makes his move. Armed with Brennan's field evidence, Genovese's
intelligence, and the political firestorm from the Sinatra photographs, he approaches the State Department with a
proposal that will change the course of the story: economic blackmail against Cuba.

### Key Beats
1. **Hartley's proposal (February 10-15)**: Hartley meets with State Department officials and lays out his case. Lucky
Luciano is operating a criminal empire from Cuba, ninety miles from Florida. He's building a heroin pipeline that will
flood American cities. The evidence is comprehensive: conference attendees, heroin routes, territory divisions, financial
projections. Hartley's proposal is bold and ruthless: threaten to cut all medical drug shipments to Cuba unless Luciano
is deported. Cuba is entirely dependent on American pharmaceuticals -- morphine, anesthesia, antibiotics. Without them,
hospitals shut down. It's not a negotiation; it's an ultimatum.
2. **The ultimatum delivered (February 21)**: The United States formally delivers its ultimatum to Cuba. Deport Luciano
or lose all medical supplies. The threat is existential -- Cuban hospitals will run out of essential drugs within weeks.
President Batista has no choice. The man who welcomed Lucky, took his money, and provided his protection is now forced to
choose between one gangster and the healthcare of an entire nation. There is no choice. Luciano must go.
3. **Brennan's call with Hartley**: Hartley calls Brennan with the news. "Your evidence made this possible, Jack. The
ultimatum worked -- Cuba's going to deport him." Brennan feels proud, validated. Months of undercover work, the risks he
took, the informants he cultivated, the alliance with Delgado -- it all paid off. He has no idea that Genovese engineered
this entire outcome. The FBN didn't just take down a mob boss; they served as Genovese's personal hit squad. Brennan was
the weapon, Hartley was the trigger, and Genovese was the hand that aimed them both.

### Character Moments
- **Director Hartley**: At his most politically masterful and morally bankrupt. The proposal is brilliant -- leveraging
medical supplies as a weapon against a sovereign nation to deport one man. Hartley doesn't care that his intelligence
came from a rival mobster staging a coup. He doesn't care that removing Lucky will empower Genovese. He cares about the
win: the biggest anti-organized-crime action in FBN history, with his name on it. His praise of Brennan is sincere but
also the final manipulation -- keeping his best agent loyal and blind.
- **Jack Brennan (via phone)**: Experiences what he believes is the culmination of his mission. His brother's death, his
undercover work in Havana, the risks and the loneliness -- it was all worth it. This moment of pride and validation is
deeply ironic: everything Brennan feels is genuine, and everything Brennan believes is wrong. He's a good man who just
did a terrible thing for the right reasons.
- **President Batista**: Forced into a corner by American leverage. The man who opened Cuba to the mob now watches as
American power closes the door. Batista's choice is no choice at all: Lucky Luciano or functioning hospitals. The
economic blackmail reveals the true power dynamic -- Cuba's sovereignty is an illusion, dependent on American goodwill
and American pharmaceuticals.
- **U.S. State Department Officials**: Represent the institutional machinery that turns a narcotics case into
geopolitical leverage. They don't care about Lucky Luciano specifically; they care about demonstrating American power and
punishing a client state that stepped out of line.

### Closing
The Act 2 climax delivers a "victory" that the audience already suspects is anything but. Lucky will be deported. Brennan
is proud. Hartley has his career-making case. Genovese has his path to the throne. And somewhere in Havana, Delgado's
warning -- "You're creating a bigger monster" -- echoes unanswered. The ultimatum is the point of no return: from here,
everything accelerates toward Lucky's arrest, the truth about Genovese, and the Pyrrhic victory that defines the film's
theme. Act 3 begins.

## FULL SCRIPT

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - HARTLEY'S OFFICE - DAY (FEBRUARY 21, 1947)

TITLE CARD: FEBRUARY 21, 1947

DIRECTOR HARTLEY stands at his window, looking down at Pennsylvania
Avenue. His office is immaculate -- American flag, framed citations,
a desk bare except for a single folder. The folder is stamped
CONFIDENTIAL.

His phone RINGS. He picks up.

                         HARTLEY
              Hartley.
                    (listens)
              Good. When did they deliver it?
                    (listens)
              This morning. Through the embassy.
              And Batista's response?

He listens. A slow, satisfied nod.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              I see. How long did it take him?

He almost smiles.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              Four hours. That's faster than I
              expected. Get me the formal
              confirmation in writing and route it
              to my desk.

He hangs up. Opens the folder. Inside: a one-page document, heavy
with official seals. He reads it with the quiet satisfaction of a man
reviewing his own masterwork.

INT. CUBAN PRESIDENTIAL PALACE - EARLIER THAT DAY

FLASHBACK - THAT MORNING

An ornate office. PRESIDENT BATISTA, 50s, sits behind a massive desk,
his military uniform pristine. Across from him, the AMERICAN
AMBASSADOR and a STATE DEPARTMENT ATTACHÉ. Both are standing.

The Ambassador places a sealed envelope on Batista's desk.

                         AMBASSADOR
              Mr. President. The United States
              government requires the immediate
              deportation of Salvatore Lucania,
              known as Lucky Luciano, from Cuban
              territory.

Batista looks at the envelope. Does not touch it.

                         BATISTA
              Señor Luciano is a legal resident of
              Cuba. He has broken no Cuban laws.

                         AMBASSADOR
              With respect, Mr. President, the
              contents of that envelope will
              clarify the United States' position.

Batista opens the envelope. Reads. His face changes. The color
drains from his cheeks, replaced by something between fury and fear.

                         BATISTA
              Medical supplies. You're threatening
              to cut medical supplies.

                         AMBASSADOR
              The United States will suspend all
              shipments of pharmaceutical products
              to Cuba -- morphine, anesthesia,
              antibiotics, surgical supplies --
              effective immediately upon failure to
              comply.

                         BATISTA
              Our hospitals --

                         AMBASSADOR
              Would experience critical shortages
              within three weeks. We understand the
              difficulty of the situation, Mr.
              President. Which is why we're
              offering a simple resolution. One
              man. That's all we're asking.

Batista stares at the document. His hands grip the edge of the desk.

                         BATISTA
              One man. In exchange for the health
              of nine million people.

                         AMBASSADOR
              A straightforward arrangement.

Batista looks up. The Ambassador meets his gaze without flinching.
The State Department Attaché studies his shoes.

                         BATISTA
              Do you have any idea what this man
              has contributed to Cuba? The
              investments, the employment, the
              revenue --

                         AMBASSADOR
              We're aware of Mr. Luciano's
              activities. That is precisely why
              we're here.

A long, terrible silence. Batista looks at the document again. The
words that will close every door Lucky Luciano was promised would
stay open.

                         BATISTA
              You leave me no choice.

                         AMBASSADOR
              That is correct, Mr. President.

Batista picks up his pen. Signs. Sets the pen down as if it weighs
a hundred pounds.

                         BATISTA
              He will be arrested and deported. You
              will have your man. And I will have
              my medical supplies.

                         AMBASSADOR
              Shipments will resume the moment
              deportation is confirmed.

The Ambassador takes the signed document. Nods.

                         AMBASSADOR (CONT'D)
              The United States appreciates Cuba's
              cooperation, Mr. President.

The word "cooperation" hangs in the air like an insult. The Americans
leave. Batista sits alone in his ornate office. He looks at his
hands -- the hands that just signed away a man's freedom because a
foreign government told him to.

He picks up his phone.

                         BATISTA
              Get me Valdés.

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - HARTLEY'S OFFICE - DAY

BACK TO PRESENT

Hartley closes the folder. Picks up the phone. Dials a long-distance
number. Waits through the clicks and pops of the international
connection.

INT. HOTEL NACIONAL - BRENNAN'S ROOM - DAY

The phone rings. BRENNAN reaches for it, notebook in hand.

                         BRENNAN
              Burke.

INTERCUT WITH HARTLEY'S OFFICE:

                         HARTLEY
              Jack. It's done.

                         BRENNAN
              What's done?

                         HARTLEY
              The ultimatum. State Department
              delivered it this morning. Cuba
              signed off four hours later. Batista
              had no choice. Luciano is going to
              be deported.

Brennan sits on the edge of his bed. The words sink in.

                         BRENNAN
              When?

                         HARTLEY
              Soon. Weeks, not months. They'll
              arrest him, detain him at Tiscornia,
              and put him on a boat back to Italy.
              Your evidence made this possible,
              Jack. The conference intelligence,
              the heroin routes, the financial
              records -- it was the foundation of
              the entire case.

                         BRENNAN
              What about the network? The pipeline
              from France? Deporting Luciano
              doesn't shut that down.

                         HARTLEY
              One thing at a time. Get the head of
              the snake and the body dies. That's
              how this works.

                         BRENNAN
              Director, my contact says --

                         HARTLEY
              Your contact is a Cuban policeman,
              Jack. He doesn't see the big picture.
              We're taking down the most powerful
              organized crime figure in the Western
              Hemisphere. This is the biggest action
              the FBN has ever taken. Your name is
              on it. Be proud of that.

Brennan is quiet for a moment.

                         BRENNAN
              I am proud.

                         HARTLEY
              You should be. Your brother -- this
              is what he would have wanted.

The mention of his brother hits like it always does. Brennan closes
his eyes briefly.

                         BRENNAN
              Yes, sir.

                         HARTLEY
              Stay in position. When the arrest
              happens, I want you there.
              Documenting. This is history, Jack.
              You're part of it.

                         BRENNAN
              Understood.

                         HARTLEY
              Good man.

The line goes dead. Brennan holds the receiver. Sets it down slowly.

He looks at his corkboard. The web of names and photographs and red
string. The case he built. The work of months.

He should feel triumph. He feels something else -- something he can't
name. He reaches for the photograph of Genovese, still facedown on
the desk where he left it weeks ago. Turns it over. Studies the face.

Then he puts it back facedown. Picks up his notebook. Starts writing.

EXT. HAVANA - MALECON SEAWALL - DUSK

Brennan walks along the Malecon. The sea is rough, waves crashing
over the wall, spray misting the air. The sun is setting, painting
the sky red and orange.

DELGADO falls into step beside him.

                         DELGADO
              I heard. The ultimatum.

                         BRENNAN
              News travels fast.

                         DELGADO
              In Cuba, everything travels fast.
              Except justice.

They walk.

                         BRENNAN
              It's done, Delgado. Luciano's going
              to be deported. The case I built --
              your information, my informants,
              all of it -- it worked.

                         DELGADO
              It worked.

                         BRENNAN
              You don't sound convinced.

                         DELGADO
              I'm convinced the Americans got what
              they wanted. I'm not convinced it's
              what Cuba needed. Or what America
              needs, for that matter.

                         BRENNAN
              We're taking down the man who built
              the French Connection. The man who's
              going to flood American cities with
              heroin.

                         DELGADO
              You're taking down the man who built
              it with rules. The man who replaces
              him won't have any.

They stop at the seawall. Waves crash below. The sky bleeds red.

                         BRENNAN
              What would you have me do? Walk away?
              My brother is dead because of
              Luciano's heroin. I came here to stop
              that. And I stopped it.

                         DELGADO
              Did you?

The question hangs between them. Brennan has no answer.

                         DELGADO (CONT'D)
              When Luciano is gone, who runs the
              pipeline? Who controls the heroin?
              It won't be nobody, Brennan. It'll
              be somebody. And the somebody who's
              been positioning himself for this
              moment since December is Vito
              Genovese.

                         BRENNAN
              Genovese is not my --

He stops himself. He's heard those words before. From Hartley.
"Genovese is not your assignment." The echo hits him. He goes quiet.

                         DELGADO
              You were about to say he's not your
              assignment.

Brennan stares at the water.

                         BRENNAN
              Yeah. I was.

A long beat. The waves. The sunset. The city behind them, going dark.

                         DELGADO
              You're a good man, Brennan. You did
              good work. But sometimes good men
              do the wrong thing for the right
              reasons. And by the time they figure
              it out, it's too late to fix.

Brennan says nothing. He looks out at the darkening ocean -- the
same ocean that will carry Lucky Luciano away from Cuba forever.

The sun drops below the horizon. The sky turns from red to purple
to black. The lights of Havana reflect on the water.

Somewhere in Washington, Hartley is celebrating. Somewhere in New
York, Genovese is waiting. And here, on the Malecon, two men who
helped bring down an empire stand in the dark and wonder what they've
built in its place.

                                                       END OF ACT TWO.

## Production Notes
- This is the Act 2 climax and should feel like a turning point -- the moment when the story's trajectory becomes
irreversible
- Hartley's State Department pitch should be staged as a power play: a man who knows he holds all the cards, presenting
an offer that can't be refused
- The phone call between Hartley and Brennan is heavy with dramatic irony: the audience knows what Brennan doesn't,
making his pride painful to watch
- Washington scenes should contrast with the Havana scenes throughout Act 2 -- cold offices and institutional power vs.
tropical beauty and street-level humanity
- The medical supply ultimatum should land with moral weight: this is a government threatening to let hospitals run dry
to deport one man
- Batista's reaction (whether shown directly or reported) should convey the helplessness of a supposedly sovereign leader
squeezed by American power
- The scene should end with forward momentum into Act 3: the victory is won, but the cost hasn't been counted yet
