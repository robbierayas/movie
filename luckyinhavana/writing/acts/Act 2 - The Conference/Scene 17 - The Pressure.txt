# Scene 17 - The Pressure

## LOCATION
EXT. - ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK / HAVANA STREETS - DAY (February 1947)

## CHARACTERS
Lucky Luciano, Frank Sinatra, Director Hartley, Press Photographers

## SCENE LAYOUT

### Opening
February 1947. Frank Sinatra returns to Havana for a four-day visit -- ostensibly vacation, but he's spending his time
with Lucky Luciano, the most wanted mob figure in America. The sun is bright, the racetracks are busy, and everywhere
Lucky and Sinatra go together, cameras follow.

### Key Beats
1. **Sinatra and Lucky in public**: Sinatra and Lucky are seen together constantly -- at Oriental Park Racetrack, in
restaurants, on the streets of Havana. They're not hiding. Lucky treats Sinatra like a friend and an asset; Sinatra
enjoys the company of powerful men. Press photographers capture everything: the two men laughing at the races, sharing
drinks, walking through crowds of admirers.
2. **The photos go public**: The photographs are published in American newspapers. The reaction is immediate and fierce.
The public is outraged -- America's most popular entertainer socializing openly with a deported mob boss, in a country
ninety miles from Florida. The press coverage transforms Lucky's presence in Cuba from an intelligence matter into a
public scandal. The political pressure becomes impossible to ignore.
3. **Hartley seizes the moment**: In Washington, Director Hartley recognizes the political opportunity. The Sinatra
photos have done what months of intelligence reports couldn't -- they've made Luciano's presence in Cuba a front-page
story. Hartley uses the scandal to pressure the State Department into action. The photographs, combined with Brennan's
evidence and Genovese's intelligence, give Hartley the leverage he needs to push for an ultimatum against Cuba.

### Character Moments
- **Lucky Luciano**: His hubris on display. Being seen with Sinatra in public is reckless -- it draws exactly the kind of
attention that an exiled mob boss should avoid. But Lucky has been in Cuba for months, untouched, powerful, treated like
royalty. He's forgotten that his safety depends on invisibility. The photographs are the beginning of the end, and he
doesn't see it coming.
- **Frank Sinatra**: A celebrity drawn to power. His presence in Havana is a convergence of entertainment and organized
crime that the American public finds intolerable. Sinatra is not a criminal, but his association with Lucky makes him a
symbol of the mob's reach into mainstream American life.
- **Director Hartley**: A political animal who knows how to use a crisis. The Sinatra photos are a gift -- they do his
lobbying for him. Public outrage creates political will, and political will creates action. Hartley has been building
toward this moment: Brennan's evidence provides the substance, the photographs provide the spectacle, and together they
create irresistible momentum.
- **Press Photographers**: Represent the force that will ultimately bring Lucky down -- not law enforcement, not the
Mafia's internal politics, but public exposure. The cameras at the racetrack are more dangerous than the gunmen at the
mansion.

### Closing
The political machinery accelerates. What was a covert intelligence operation is now a public demand for action. Hartley
has his weapon: a scandal too big to ignore, evidence too detailed to dismiss, and a political climate that demands
results. The scene ends with the sense that the walls are closing in on Lucky -- not from enemies within the Mafia, but
from the one force even the mob can't buy: public outrage.

## FULL SCRIPT

EXT. ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - GRANDSTAND - DAY (FEBRUARY 1947)

TITLE CARD: FEBRUARY 1947

Blazing Caribbean sun. The grandstand is packed with SPECTATORS in
white suits and Panama hats. HORSES thunder down the home stretch.
The crowd roars.

LUCKY LUCIANO stands at the rail of the Jockey Club section, elevated
above the general crowd. Beside him, FRANK SINATRA, 31, tanned, in
a blue sport coat and open collar. They look like two friends at the
races. They look like kings.

                         SINATRA
              I got a hundred on the four horse.
              Tell me I'm gonna win, Charlie.

                         LUCKY
              You're gonna win, Frank.

The four horse pulls ahead. Sinatra pumps his fist. Laughs.

                         SINATRA
              How do you do that?

                         LUCKY
              I don't do anything. The horse does
              the running.

                         SINATRA
              Yeah, but somehow the horse always
              runs your way.

Lucky smiles. A PRESS PHOTOGRAPHER across the track raises his camera.
FLASH. Lucky and Sinatra, laughing at the races.

Neither man notices.

EXT. HAVANA STREET - DAY

Lucky and Sinatra walk down a sun-drenched Havana boulevard. DELGADO
follows three paces behind. ADMIRERS wave. A WOMAN asks Sinatra for
an autograph. He obliges, grinning.

A PHOTOGRAPHER steps from behind a parked car. FLASH. Lucky and
Sinatra, side by side, walking through the crowd.

                         SINATRA
              You know what I love about this
              place? Nobody cares. In New York, I
              can't walk down the street without
              somebody writing about it. Here --
              people just want to shake your hand.

                         LUCKY
              That's Cuba, Frank. Everybody's
              welcome here.

Behind them, Delgado watches the photographer with uneasy eyes.

EXT. HAVANA RESTAURANT - PATIO - DAY

Lucky and Sinatra at a table on a restaurant patio. Drinks, cigars,
the Havana skyline behind them. Two PHOTOGRAPHERS across the street,
cameras trained on the patio.

FLASH. FLASH.

Delgado approaches Lucky's shoulder.

                         DELGADO
                    (low)
              The photographers. There are more
              today than yesterday. American press.

                         LUCKY
              Let them take pictures. I'm having
              lunch with a friend. There's no law
              against it.

                         DELGADO
              In Cuba, no. But those photographs go
              back to the States.

Lucky waves it off. Turns back to Sinatra.

                         LUCKY
              Frank, you worry too much.

                         SINATRA
              I'm not worried. Should I be worried?

                         LUCKY
              Nobody should be worried. We're in
              paradise.

Sinatra raises his glass. Lucky raises his. They drink. FLASH. The
photographs pile up.

INT. FBN HEADQUARTERS - HARTLEY'S OFFICE - DAY

A different world. Cold fluorescent light. DIRECTOR HARTLEY sits
behind his desk. Before him: a spread of NEWSPAPER CLIPPINGS.
Front-page photographs -- Lucky Luciano and Frank Sinatra at the
races. At restaurants. Walking the streets of Havana. The headlines
scream: "SINATRA PALS WITH DEPORTED GANGSTER" ... "LUCIANO LIVING
LARGE IN CUBA" ... "AMERICA'S MOST WANTED: 90 MILES FROM FLORIDA."

Hartley examines each clipping with the appreciation of a man who's
been handed exactly what he needed.

A KNOCK. An AIDE enters.

                         AIDE
              Director, the Associated Press is
              running a wire story. Sinatra and
              Luciano. It's going to be in every
              paper in the country by tomorrow.

                         HARTLEY
              I know.

                         AIDE
              The Secretary of State's office
              called. They want a briefing.

                         HARTLEY
              I'll bet they do. Set it up.
              Tomorrow morning. And pull together
              everything we have on Luciano's Cuba
              operations -- Brennan's reports, the
              conference intelligence, the heroin
              routes. All of it.

                         AIDE
              The full file?

                         HARTLEY
              The full file. It's time.

The aide leaves. Hartley looks at the newspaper photographs one more
time. Lucky Luciano laughing in the sun. Frank Sinatra beside him.

Hartley picks up his phone. Dials.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              Get me the Undersecretary for
              Western Hemisphere Affairs.

He waits. His fingers tap the desk, patient, rhythmic.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              Bill. It's Hartley. I assume you've
              seen the papers... Yes. I have a
              proposal for you. Something that will
              solve your Luciano problem and mine
              at the same time. How soon can we
              meet?

He smiles. The smile of a man whose moment has arrived.

INT. STATE DEPARTMENT - CONFERENCE ROOM - DAY

A wood-paneled room. American flags. HARTLEY sits across a polished
table from three STATE DEPARTMENT OFFICIALS. He has a thick folder
in front of him.

                         HARTLEY
              Gentlemen. You've seen the
              photographs. America's biggest
              celebrity photographed arm in arm
              with America's most notorious
              gangster, in a country ninety miles
              from Florida. The public reaction has
              been... considerable.

                         STATE DEPT. OFFICIAL
              The phones haven't stopped ringing.
              Senators, congressmen, newspapers.
              They all want to know why a deported
              criminal is living like a king in
              Cuba.

                         HARTLEY
              Because he is living like a king in
              Cuba. And it's worse than the photos
              suggest.

He opens the folder. Spreads documents across the table.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              My agent in Havana has documented a
              complete narcotics infrastructure.
              Luciano hosted the largest organized
              crime conference in American history
              last month. Twenty-three bosses.
              They've built a heroin pipeline from
              Turkey through France to Cuba --
              projected revenue, fifty million
              dollars a year. Cuba is the hub. And
              Luciano is running it all from a
              mansion in Miramar.

The officials pass the documents between them. Their faces tighten.

                         STATE DEPT. OFFICIAL
              What are you proposing?

                         HARTLEY
              An ultimatum.

He lets the word hang.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              Cuba is entirely dependent on
              American medical supplies. Morphine.
              Anesthesia. Antibiotics. All of it
              comes from us. Without American
              pharmaceuticals, Cuban hospitals shut
              down within weeks.

He leans forward.

                         HARTLEY (CONT'D)
              We tell Cuba: deport Luciano, or we
              cut everything. Every shipment.
              Every supply line. Their hospitals go
              dark until he's on a boat.

The room is quiet.

                         STATE DEPT. OFFICIAL
              That's... aggressive.

                         HARTLEY
              The alternative is explaining to the
              American public why their government
              can't stop a convicted gangster from
              running a drug empire in our backyard.
              The photographs have made this a
              political crisis. I'm offering a
              political solution.

The officials exchange glances. The weight of it settles.

                         STATE DEPT. OFFICIAL
              Draft the language. We'll take it
              upstairs.

Hartley nods. Closes his folder. Stands.

                         HARTLEY
              Gentlemen. By March, Lucky Luciano
              will be on a boat back to Italy. I
              guarantee it.

He walks out. The door closes behind him. The officials sit in
silence, looking at the photographs of Lucky and Sinatra laughing in
the Cuban sun.

EXT. ORIENTAL PARK RACETRACK - GRANDSTAND - DAY

Back in Havana. Another day at the races. Lucky and Sinatra at the
rail, surrounded by ADMIRERS. The sun blazes. The horses run.

FLASH. FLASH. FLASH.

The photographers keep shooting. Lucky doesn't look at them. He's
watching the horses. Laughing with Sinatra. Holding court.

He has no idea that every photograph is a nail in his coffin. That
every smile is evidence. That the paradise he built is being
dismantled in a wood-paneled room four thousand miles away.

The horses cross the finish line. The crowd cheers. Lucky raises
his glass.

And the cameras keep flashing.

## Production Notes
- The Sinatra-Lucky scenes should feel glamorous and reckless: two famous men enjoying themselves in public, oblivious to
the consequences
- The press photographers should feel like a building threat -- each flash of a camera is a nail in Lucky's coffin
- Cross-cutting between Havana glamour and Washington political maneuvering creates dramatic contrast
- Oriental Park Racetrack scenes should capture 1940s Havana at its most vibrant: crowds, horses, money, sunshine
- Hartley's Washington scenes should feel like the machinery of power grinding into motion -- offices, phone calls,
meetings
- The tonal shift: Havana is bright and carefree, Washington is cold and calculated
