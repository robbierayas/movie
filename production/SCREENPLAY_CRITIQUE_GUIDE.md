## Core Principles

### The Cinematic Eye
Screenplays are written for a visual medium. Great critique requires "a keen third cinematic eye" that can visualize the story as if the film was already playing. Don't judge scripts on literary merits alone—evaluate how scenes will translate to screen.

### Objectivity
Set aside personal biases and judge scripts against industry standards. A horror screenplay should be evaluated on horror genre expectations, not your personal taste in genres.

### Empathy
Remember the writer has invested significant time and energy. Approach feedback with respect. Critique the script, not the writer.

## The Four Pillars of Screenplay Analysis

### 1. Characters
Characters are the heart of any screenplay—the vessels through which audiences experience the story.

**Evaluate:**
- **Motivation**: Are character goals clear and compelling?
- **Consistency**: Do characters act according to established traits?
- **Complexity**: Do they have depth, including flaws and virtues?
- **Voice**: Does each character have a distinct way of speaking?
- **Agency**: Are characters active (shaping the story) or passive (being shaped by it)?
- **Arc**: Do main characters change/grow through the story?
- **Relationships**: How do characters influence each other?

### 2. Conflict
Conflict is the engine that propels the story forward.

**Evaluate:**
- **Clarity**: Is the central conflict well-defined?
- **Stakes**: What do characters stand to lose?
- **Escalation**: Does tension increase throughout?
- **Resolution**: Is the conflict adequately resolved?
- **Emotional Impact**: Does the conflict generate genuine emotional responses?

### 3. Structure (Chronology)
The framework that holds the screenplay together.

**Evaluate:**
- **Three-Act Structure**:
  - Act 1 (Setup): Introduces characters and conflict
  - Act 2 (Confrontation): Develops conflict through obstacles
  - Act 3 (Resolution): Concludes and resolves the conflict
- **Pacing**: Does the story move at an appropriate speed?
- **Scene Progression**: Does each scene build logically on the previous?
- **Plot Points**: Are key turning points effectively placed?
- **Subplots**: Do they enhance the main story?

### 4. Theme
The underlying message or central idea the writer wants to convey.

**Evaluate:**
- **Clarity**: Is the theme identifiable?
- **Integration**: Does theme emerge naturally through character actions (not preachy)?
- **Universality**: Does it resonate with human experience?
- **Consistency**: Is the thematic message reinforced throughout?

## Detailed Evaluation Categories

### Story/Plot
- Is there a clear and compelling story arc?
- Are there plot holes or logical inconsistencies?
- Does the story hold attention throughout?
- Is the ending satisfying and earned?

### Dialogue
- Does it sound natural and authentic?
- Does each character have a distinct voice?
- Does dialogue advance the plot?
- Is there effective subtext (hidden meanings)?
- Is exposition handled gracefully or does it feel forced?

#### Dialogue Length and Rhythm

There's no correct line length. The question is whether the rhythm serves the scene.

**Short, punchy exchanges work when:**
- Characters are in conflict or disagreement
- Action is happening simultaneously
- Tension is high and time is short
- Characters know each other well and use shorthand

**Longer lines work when:**
- A character is explaining, persuading, or confessing
- Emotional weight needs space to land
- The speaker has power/control in the scene
- Information must be delivered clearly

**The problem isn't length—it's monotony.** If every exchange is 1-2 lines back and forth, it becomes a tennis match. If every line is a 4-sentence paragraph, it becomes speeches. Vary the rhythm based on what the moment needs.

#### Relationship-Specific Dialogue

Different relationships require different dialogue approaches:

**Parent/Child:**
- Shows how they parent (protective, distant, warm, strict)
- Uses nicknames, pet names, or formal names intentionally
- Has unequal power dynamic visible in who asks vs. who tells
- Reveals history through shorthand references
- **Critical:** If the parent loves the child, we need to SEE it in how they speak to them—not just be told

**Strangers:**
- More formal, complete sentences
- Questions to gather information
- Politeness or guardedness

**Old Friends/Partners:**
- Interrupting, finishing sentences
- Inside jokes or references
- Comfortable silence or shorthand
- Can disagree without explaining why

**Enemies/Rivals:**
- Subtext loaded (saying one thing, meaning another)
- Jabs disguised as politeness
- What's NOT said matters more

#### What Makes Dialogue Entertaining

Dialogue entertains when it has:

1. **Conflict** - Characters want different things in the conversation. Even friendly dialogue has micro-tensions.

2. **Subtext** - What they mean vs. what they say. "I'm fine" when clearly not fine.

3. **Character voice** - A line only THIS character would say this way.

4. **Reveals** - Each exchange tells us something new (about character, plot, or relationship).

5. **Rhythm variation** - Short bursts, then a longer beat. One character dominates, then the other pushes back.

**Boring dialogue** is when:
- Characters agree too easily
- Everyone speaks in the same rhythm
- Lines exist only to convey information
- There's no push-and-pull
- You could swap speakers and nothing changes

#### The Relationship Gap Problem

If a relationship matters to the plot, dialogue must establish it—not just mention it.

**Problem example:** Sarah loves her kids, but we only see her giving instructions and being worried. No warmth, no specific connection, no moments that show WHY this relationship matters beyond biology.

**Fix:** Add at least one exchange that shows the specific texture of this relationship:
- An inside joke between them
- A nickname with history
- A moment of genuine connection before danger separates them
- How she speaks to THESE specific children vs. how she'd speak to any child

If the audience should care about a relationship, they need to experience it, not just be informed of it.

### Pacing
- Is the story too slow or too rushed?
- Are scene lengths appropriate?
- Are transitions smooth?
- Are cliffhangers and reveals strategically placed?

### Tone
- Is the tone consistent throughout?
- Is it appropriate for the genre?
- Do tonal shifts feel intentional?

### Setting/World-Building
- Is the world believable and immersive?
- Are settings used effectively for storytelling?
- Is the world consistent in its rules?

### Visuals
- Are there striking, memorable visual moments?
- Is "show don't tell" employed effectively?
- Could any dialogue be replaced with visual storytelling?

### Formatting
- Does it follow standard screenplay format?
- Are action lines concise and visual?
- Is it the appropriate length? (1 page ≈ 1 minute)

## Coverage Ratings

Professional script coverage uses these standard ratings:

| Rating | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Recommend** | Outstanding script ready for production consideration |
| **Strong Consider** | Very good with minor issues; worth serious attention |
| **Consider** | Promising but needs work; has potential |
| **Consider with Reservations** | Some merit but significant issues |
| **Pass** | Not ready; needs substantial revision |

**Industry Reality:**
- ~95% of scripts receive a Pass
- ~4% receive a Consider
- ~1% receive a Recommend

## Step-by-Step Analysis Process

### First Read
1. Read the entire script without taking notes
2. Experience it as an audience would
3. Note your emotional responses
4. Form an overall impression

### Second Read (Analytical)
1. **Structure Analysis**
   - Identify where each act begins and ends
   - Note key plot points and turning points
   - Break down acts into sequences
   - Identify subplots

2. **Character Analysis**
   - Track each main character's arc
   - Note motivations and goals
   - Evaluate consistency of actions
   - Assess relationship dynamics

3. **Scene-by-Scene Review**
   - Does each scene advance plot or character?
   - Are entrances and exits motivated?
   - Is each scene necessary?

4. **Dialogue Pass**
   - Is each character's voice distinct?
   - Identify clunky exposition
   - Note standout dialogue moments

5. **Theme Identification**
   - What is the screenplay saying?
   - How is theme expressed through action?
   - Are there recurring symbols or motifs?

## Giving Effective Feedback

### Do
- Read thoroughly before giving notes
- Focus on structure first, details later
- Be specific and actionable
- Explain WHY something doesn't work
- Suggest possible solutions
- Balance praise with criticism
- Keep feedback script-focused, not personal
- Be respectful and supportive

### Don't
- Give vague criticism ("this doesn't work")
- Attack the writer personally
- Rewrite the script for them
- Overwhelm with too many notes
- Focus only on negatives
- Compare to other scripts

### Types of Notes
- **Coverage**: Summary evaluation with rating
- **Development Notes**: Creative suggestions for story, characters, dialogue
- **Writer-to-Writer Notes**: Peer feedback from fellow writers
- **Line Edits**: Grammar, spelling, formatting corrections

## Common Screenplay Problems

### Structure Issues
- No clear inciting incident
- Saggy second act
- Rushed or unearned ending
- Subplots that don't connect to main story
- Scenes that don't advance anything

### Character Issues
- Protagonist is passive/reactive
- Characters lack clear motivation
- All characters sound the same
- Underdeveloped supporting characters
- Character decisions feel arbitrary

### Dialogue Issues
- On-the-nose dialogue (saying exactly what they mean)
- Expository dialogue ("As you know, Bob...")
- All characters have same voice
- Dialogue that doesn't reveal character
- Too much talking, not enough action

### Pacing Issues
- Slow opening that loses the reader
- Action scenes that run too long
- Repetitive scenes
- Insufficient rising action
- Climax comes too early or too late

### Immersion-Breaking Moments
When character actions contradict fundamental human behavior, the audience stops watching the story and starts questioning the writing. These are critical problems that must be addressed.

**Common immersion breaks:**
- A parent not prioritizing children's safety during danger
- A character ignoring obvious solutions without explanation
- Emotional responses that don't match the situation (not grieving a death, not celebrating a victory)
- Characters who should be enemies cooperating without tension
- Knowledge characters shouldn't have (unless explained)

**The test:** If the audience asks "Why would they do that?" and there's no answer in the script, it's an immersion break.

**Fixes require one of:**
1. **Establish the exception** - Show why this character would act differently than expected ("Kids are already safe with my sister")
2. **Acknowledge the contradiction** - Have other characters question it ("What about your children?") forcing the character to justify
3. **Use the contradiction** - Make it a character flaw that has consequences

## Critique Scope: What IS and ISN'T Valid Feedback

### Valid Critique Targets
- Character motivations that don't justify their actions
- Plot points that require audience to ignore logic
- Emotional beats that aren't earned
- Unclear story information the audience needs
- Tone inconsistencies that confuse genre expectations

### NOT Valid Critique Targets
- **Runtime/length concerns** - That's a production issue, not a story issue. If scenes serve the narrative, they belong.
- **Demanding explanation scenes for everything** - Relationships and dynamics CAN be implied through efficient dialogue. Not every character needs a backstory scene.
- **Personal preference for hopeful endings** - Bleak/nihilistic endings are valid if structurally sound. Hope that builds only to be crushed IS a payoff, not a problem.
- **Wanting to see everything on screen** - Surprise reveals are valid. Setup can be subtle (glances, hints) with payoff later.

### The Key Distinction: Foundational vs. Efficient

**Foundational elements MUST be clear** (regardless of time):
- Why the protagonist pursues their goal
- Why the antagonist opposes them
- What's at stake if they fail
- Why characters make major plot-driving decisions

**Efficient elements CAN be implied**:
- How long characters have known each other
- The specifics of their shared history
- Supporting characters' full backgrounds
- Relationships that aren't central to plot

**Example:** You don't need a scene showing two characters becoming friends if their dialogue reflects an established friendship. But if one betrays the other, the motivation for betrayal must be clear—that's foundational.

## Surprise Reveals vs. Convenient Solutions

A surprise should feel inevitable in retrospect. A convenience feels like the writer needed something to happen.

### Satisfying Surprise Setup
- **Concept is established** - The audience knows the thing is possible
- **Character capability is shown** - We've seen them do similar things
- **Hints exist on rewatch** - Glances, half-lines, behavior that makes sense later
- **The surprise fits character** - It's something they WOULD do

### Convenient Solution (Problem)
- No prior indication the solution existed
- Character suddenly has skills/resources never mentioned
- Timing is too perfect without justification
- Contradicts established limitations

**Test:** Could you rewatch and see the setup? If yes, it's a surprise. If no, it's a convenience.

## Evaluation Checklist

Use this checklist when reviewing a screenplay:

### Story/Concept
- [ ] Is the concept original or a fresh take?
- [ ] Is there a clear logline?
- [ ] Does the story have a beginning, middle, and end?
- [ ] Are the stakes clear and compelling?

### Characters
- [ ] Is the protagonist active and driving the story?
- [ ] Do main characters have clear goals?
- [ ] Do characters have distinct voices?
- [ ] Are character arcs complete and satisfying?
- [ ] Is the antagonist well-developed?

### Structure
- [ ] Is Act 1 setup efficient (roughly 25%)?
- [ ] Does Act 2 have sufficient conflict and escalation (roughly 50%)?
- [ ] Is Act 3 resolution satisfying (roughly 25%)?
- [ ] Are major plot points well-placed?
- [ ] Does each scene earn its place?

### Dialogue
- [ ] Is dialogue authentic to each character?
- [ ] Is exposition handled gracefully?
- [ ] Is there effective subtext?
- [ ] Does dialogue advance plot or reveal character?

### Visual Storytelling
- [ ] Does it "show" more than "tell"?
- [ ] Are action lines concise and visual?
- [ ] Are there memorable visual moments?

### Theme
- [ ] Is there a clear thematic through-line?
- [ ] Is theme expressed through action, not preaching?

### Technical
- [ ] Proper screenplay format?
- [ ] Appropriate page count for genre?
- [ ] Clean, readable pages?

### Overall
- [ ] Would you watch this movie?
- [ ] Does it evoke emotional response?
- [ ] Is it producible?
